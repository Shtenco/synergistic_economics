## Abstract

This volume reconstructs major traditions in economic thought as competing and complementary research programmes. It does not attempt to average their conclusions or to assemble a catalogue of famous economists. It asks what each tradition assumes about people, knowledge, value, production, institutions, money, power, time and nature; what mechanisms it explains; what its mathematical core makes visible; what it leaves outside the model; and under which conditions its conclusions are likely to hold.

The central methodological proposition is that economic schools should be compared at the level of **assumptions, mechanisms, boundary conditions and observable implications**. Apparent contradictions may be genuine, may arise from different levels of analysis, or may reflect different time horizons and institutional environments. A scientific synthesis must distinguish these cases.

The volume develops:
- a protocol for comparing theories without collapsing their differences;
- a map of major economic schools and their formal cores;
- a set of cross-school tensions and conditional reconciliation hypotheses;
- a preliminary integrated model of production, exchange, finance, institutions, ecology and prosperity;
- draft axioms for SYNERGISTIC ECONOMICS;
- falsifiable propositions and a research programme for empirical and computational testing.

SYNERGY is not presented as an already validated replacement for existing economics. It is a proposed integrative research programme. Its novelty, if any, must be established by explanatory power, predictive performance, institutional feasibility and reproducible evidence—not by terminology or technological ambition.

# Part I — How to conduct a scientific synthesis

## 1. The object of comparison

An economic school is not just a list of policy preferences. It is a connected set of propositions about:

1. **Ontology:** what entities exist in the economy—individuals, firms, classes, institutions, networks, assets, ecosystems?
2. **Human behaviour:** how actors choose, learn, cooperate, compete and respond to incentives.
3. **Knowledge:** what information exists, who possesses it, how it is communicated and what remains unknowable.
4. **Value and production:** how usefulness, prices, output, surplus, productivity and wealth arise.
5. **Coordination:** how plans and activities are reconciled across actors.
6. **Distribution and power:** who owns assets, bears risk, controls decisions and receives residual claims.
7. **Money and finance:** how payment, credit, liquidity, leverage and financial instability work.
8. **Time and change:** how investment, innovation, cycles, expectations and path dependence operate.
9. **Nature and reproduction:** how human, produced, institutional and natural assets are maintained or depleted.
10. **Method:** what counts as explanation, evidence, measurement and refutation.

Two theories cannot be compared fairly if one is a model of short-run price adjustment and the other is a theory of long-run institutional transformation. The unit of comparison must therefore include the question, scale, time horizon and institutional setting.

## 2. The comparison protocol

Every school in this volume is assessed through the same template:

- **Core question:** the problem the tradition primarily addresses.
- **Analytical unit:** individual, household, firm, class, market, state, institution, network or system.
- **Behavioural assumptions:** rationality, bounded rationality, habit, power, cooperation and strategic action.
- **Knowledge assumptions:** complete, local, dispersed, tacit, asymmetric or uncertain information.
- **Mechanism:** the causal process linking conditions to outcomes.
- **Mathematical core:** a representative model or formal relationship. It is not claimed that every thinker in a tradition used this exact equation.
- **Explanatory achievements:** questions for which the framework is useful.
- **Boundary conditions and limitations:** where assumptions are restrictive or mechanisms incomplete.
- **Cross-school tension:** which other traditions emphasize a different mechanism.
- **SYNERGY translation:** what can be retained, modified, combined or tested.

A school is not refuted merely because its model is simplified. Simplification is useful when it isolates a mechanism. The relevant question is whether the omitted factors materially change the conclusion in the domain where the model is applied.

## 3. Three types of apparent contradiction

**Type A — Logical incompatibility.** Two claims cannot both be true under the same definitions, assumptions, institutional conditions and time horizon. Example: a strict claim that a given allocation is uniquely optimal versus a claim that a different allocation is uniquely optimal under the same objective and constraints.

**Type B — Conditional difference.** Both claims may be true under different conditions. A market may coordinate standardized, contestable goods effectively, while collective governance may be superior for a shared resource with difficult exclusion and local monitoring.

**Type C — Level or horizon difference.** A proposition about an individual firm may not imply the same result for the macroeconomy; a short-run stabilisation result may not imply a long-run growth result. Aggregation, feedback and composition effects matter.

The synthesis must not erase Type A conflicts by vague language. It must specify which proposition is retained, revised or rejected and why. Type B conflicts become conditional mechanism-selection rules. Type C conflicts require a multilevel, dynamic model.

## 4. Standards of evidence

Claims in the research programme must be labelled:

- **Historical interpretation:** a reading of a thinker or school, supported by primary and scholarly sources.
- **Established analytical result:** a result derived under stated assumptions.
- **Empirical regularity:** a pattern supported by data in a specified domain.
- **Design principle:** a normative or institutional commitment.
- **SYNERGY hypothesis:** a new proposition that remains to be tested.
- **Implementation claim:** a statement about software or institutions actually built and verified.

A simulation demonstrates what follows from its assumptions and code; it does not by itself demonstrate what will happen in a real economy. Every simulation should disclose parameters, initial conditions, behavioural rules, random seeds, calibration data, validation tests and competing baselines.

# Part II — Comparative analysis of economic traditions

## 5. Ancient, scholastic and early political economy

### 5.1 Ancient and scholastic traditions

**Core question.** How should households, communities and polities organize provisioning, exchange, property, justice and obligations?

**Contributions.** These traditions connect economic activity to ethics, political authority, household reproduction and the common good. Aristotle distinguished use and exchange and examined the purposes and limits of acquisition. Scholastic writers examined just price, usury, property, obligation and moral limits on exchange. Ibn Khaldun analysed labour, taxation, social cohesion, state power and the rise and decline of dynasties.

**Formal core.** There is no single canonical mathematical model. A useful modern reconstruction treats economic conduct as constrained choice under ethical, legal and social rules:
\[
\max_{a\in A_i} U_i(a) \quad \text{s.t. } a\in\mathcal L,\; \mathcal R,\; \mathcal N
\]
where \(\mathcal L\), \(\mathcal R\) and \(\mathcal N\) represent legal, rights-based and normative constraints.

**Achievement.** They prevent the category error of treating every profitable transaction as socially legitimate or every economic question as reducible to price.

**Limitations.** Ethical concepts can be difficult to operationalize; historical institutions and theological premises cannot be transferred unchanged to modern plural societies.

**SYNERGY translation.** Economic efficiency is evaluated within explicit rights, legitimacy and social-reproduction constraints, rather than treated as a complete moral criterion.

## 6. Mercantilism and physiocracy

### 6.1 Mercantilist traditions

**Core question.** How can a state build fiscal, commercial and strategic capacity in a world of geopolitical competition?

**Mechanisms.** Trade policy, shipping, monetary and fiscal institutions, strategic industries, colonial extraction and state-supported commerce.

**Formal reconstruction.** A national balance-sheet perspective:
\[
\Delta NFA = CA + valuation\ effects
\]
where net foreign assets change through the current account and valuation effects. This is not a complete mercantilist model, but it helps distinguish trade flows, financial claims and domestic productive capacity.

**Achievement.** Attention to state capacity, external constraints, strategic dependencies and the institutional foundations of commerce.

**Limitations.** Equating national prosperity with bullion or persistent trade surpluses is misleading; trade is not necessarily zero-sum, and protection can entrench rent-seeking and inefficient incumbents.

**Tension.** Classical trade theory emphasizes mutual gains from specialization; strategic-trade and development arguments emphasize learning, security and coordination failures.

**SYNERGY translation.** Measure resilience and strategic capability alongside consumer surplus and productivity; require transparent evidence before granting protection or subsidy.

### 6.2 Physiocracy

**Core question.** What is the source and circulation of a society's net product?

**Mechanism.** Quesnay's Tableau économique represented circular flows among productive, proprietary and “sterile” classes, with agriculture treated as the source of the net product in the historical model.

**Formal reconstruction.**
\[
Y = C + I + G + NX, \qquad
\text{and sectoral flows must satisfy input-output balance.}
\]
The modern input-output identity generalizes the circular-flow insight; it does not validate the physiocratic claim that only agriculture is productive.

**Achievement.** The economy is a reproduction system of interdependent flows, not merely a collection of isolated exchanges.

**Limitations.** The historical sectoral theory of productive surplus is too narrow for industrial, service, knowledge and digital economies.

**SYNERGY translation.** Build a multi-sector flow model that tracks intermediate inputs, final output, maintenance, ecological throughput and net additions to productive capacity.

## 7. Classical political economy

### 7.1 Adam Smith and division of labour

**Core question.** How do specialization, exchange, competition and institutions expand productivity and the wealth of nations?

**Mechanism.** Division of labour increases dexterity, saves time and encourages machinery; market extent constrains the gains from specialization. Smith also recognized the importance of justice, public works, education and institutional conditions.

**Representative formal core.**
\[
Q = A\,F(K,L), \qquad
\text{and } A=A(s,m,\tau,\kappa)
\]
where productivity \(A\) may depend on specialization \(s\), market extent \(m\), technology \(\tau\) and institutional conditions \(\kappa\). This is a modern stylization, not Smith's own equation.

**Achievement.** Explains how specialization and exchange can increase output and why market size, institutions and public infrastructure matter.

**Limitations.** Division of labour can create dependency, monotony, coordination bottlenecks and unequal bargaining power. The “invisible hand” is not a theorem that all self-interested conduct always maximizes social welfare.

**Cross-school tension.** Smithian specialization complements Ricardo's comparative advantage, but Marx and institutionalists ask who controls the production process and captures its gains.

**SYNERGY translation.** Encourage specialization and open exchange while tracking control, worker capabilities, dependency, externalities and the distribution of productivity gains.

### 7.2 Ricardo, comparative advantage and distribution

**Core question.** How do relative productivity differences shape trade and the distribution of income among wages, profits and rents?

**Representative trade model.** With two goods and two countries, comparative advantage follows from relative opportunity costs, not absolute productivity:
\[
OC_A(X)=\frac{a_{LX}}{a_{LY}},\qquad
OC_B(X)=\frac{b_{LX}}{b_{LY}}.
\]
Country A has comparative advantage in X if its opportunity cost of X is lower.

**Achievement.** Demonstrates the potential gains from specialization and trade even when one party is absolutely more productive in every activity.

**Limitations.** The simplest model abstracts from adjustment costs, distributional conflict, unemployment, market power, transport, strategic dependence, capital mobility and environmental costs.

**Tension.** Aggregate gains from trade do not imply that every worker, region or firm gains. Kaldorian, structuralist and political-economy approaches emphasize uneven adjustment and development paths.

**SYNERGY translation.** Evaluate trade gains net of transition costs, resilience, environmental impact and compensation or capability-building for adversely affected participants.

### 7.3 Malthus, Say and classical macro questions

Malthus emphasized population dynamics, demand deficiencies and the possibility that output may not automatically find sufficient effective demand. Say's Law is often summarized as supply creating its own demand, but historical interpretations are more nuanced than the slogan.

A useful accounting distinction is:
\[
Y \equiv C+I+G+NX
\]
as an ex post national-accounting identity, which does **not** prove that planned expenditure will always be sufficient to purchase potential output. The distinction between accounting identity and causal theory is essential.

**SYNERGY translation.** Keep production capacity, realized output, planned expenditure, inventories and effective demand separate in the system model.

## 8. Marxian and socialist political economy

### 8.1 Marxian analysis

**Core question.** How do ownership of the means of production, wage labour, accumulation, competition and class relations shape production and distribution?

**Representative formal core.** In a simplified value-accounting notation:
\[
c+v+s
\]
where \(c\) is constant capital advanced, \(v\) variable capital advanced and \(s\) surplus value in the theory's own conceptual framework. The rate of surplus value is:
\[
e=\frac{s}{v}.
\]
For a simplified reproduction scheme, interdepartmental balance conditions constrain the exchange of means of production and consumption goods. These are theoretical categories within Marxian analysis and should not be conflated with national accounts or ordinary accounting profit.

**Achievement.** Directs attention to ownership, control, surplus appropriation, accumulation, bargaining power, crises and the social organization of production.

**Limitations and debates.** The labour theory of value and transformation problem remain contested; the relation between value categories and observed prices is debated. Class analysis can under-specify heterogeneity within classes, entrepreneurship, consumer valuation, institutional variation and non-capitalist forms of coordination. Historical socialist planning outcomes cannot be reduced to one cause.

**Cross-school tension.** Subjective-value and marginalist theories explain relative prices through scarcity and preferences; Marxian theory emphasizes social relations of production and surplus. They may address different explanatory questions, but become incompatible when both are asserted as a single exclusive theory of the same price phenomenon.

**SYNERGY translation.** Separate the measurement of market prices, production costs, surplus, ownership rights and bargaining power. Investigate who controls residual claims and whether contributors can gain durable participation in productive assets.

### 8.2 Cooperative, mutualist and socialist traditions

These traditions examine worker ownership, mutual credit, common ownership, democratic firms, social provision and alternative forms of investment.

**Representative cooperative objective.**
\[
\max \; W_{members}=\sum_i \omega_i U_i
\]
subject to enterprise solvency, member rights and market constraints. Different cooperative models choose different weights, membership rules and surplus-allocation principles.

**Achievement.** They show that firms need not have a single investor-owner structure and that democratic control can be combined with market exchange.

**Limitations.** Cooperatives can face capital constraints, governance costs, free-riding, insider-outsider conflicts, underinvestment and difficulty scaling democratic participation. These are design challenges, not proof of inevitable failure.

**SYNERGY translation.** Treat cooperative ownership as one organizational option; compare it with investor-owned firms, partnerships, public enterprises and hybrid structures using common performance measures.

## 9. Marginalist and neoclassical economics

### 9.1 Marginal utility and choice

**Core question.** How do preferences, scarcity and constraints shape individual choices and market demand?

**Representative formal core.**
\[
\max_{x\geq0} U(x) \quad \text{s.t. } p\cdot x\leq m.
\]
At an interior optimum, marginal rates of substitution equal relative prices:
\[
\frac{MU_i}{MU_j}=\frac{p_i}{p_j}.
\]

**Achievement.** Provides a precise framework for trade-offs, consumer choice, substitution, demand and welfare analysis.

**Limitations.** Results depend on preference assumptions, information, feasible sets and market structure. Utility is not automatically measurable interpersonal welfare; revealed preference does not settle questions of rights, power or capability.

**SYNERGY translation.** Use constrained optimization for specific decisions, but avoid treating a single utility function as a complete social objective.

### 9.2 General equilibrium and welfare economics

**Core question.** Under what conditions can decentralized markets clear and achieve Pareto-efficient allocations?

**Representative formal core.** An equilibrium price vector \(p^*\) and allocation \(x^*\) satisfy household optimization, firm optimization and market clearing:
\[
\sum_i x_i(p^*,m_i)=\sum_j y_j(p^*)+\bar e.
\]

The First Welfare Theorem requires demanding assumptions, including appropriate competitive conditions and absence of relevant externalities; the Second Welfare Theorem depends on convexity and redistribution through suitable initial endowments.

**Achievement.** Clarifies the conditions under which price-mediated decentralization can coordinate plans and why externalities, public goods, market power and missing markets matter.

**Limitations.** Existence is not the same as uniqueness, stability, fairness or practical attainability. Pareto efficiency cannot rank all distributions; an allocation can be Pareto-efficient and deeply unequal.

**Cross-school tension.** Hayek emphasizes the discovery process and dispersed knowledge; general-equilibrium theory often represents equilibrium as a simultaneous system. These are not direct logical opposites, but they ask different questions about process, information and attainability.

**SYNERGY translation.** Use equilibrium as a benchmark, not a universal description. Test transition paths, disequilibrium, power, institutional feasibility and distribution.

### 9.3 Marshallian partial equilibrium

**Core question.** How do supply and demand interact in a particular market, with other factors temporarily held constant?

**Representative formal core.**
\[
Q_d(p;z)=Q_s(p;w,\tau),\qquad Q_d=Q_s.
\]
Elasticities and time horizons determine how adjustment occurs.

**Achievement.** Practical analysis of specific markets, taxes, price controls, costs and time-dependent supply responses.

**Limitations.** Ceteris paribus assumptions can fail when linked markets, income, expectations or network effects change materially.

**SYNERGY translation.** Use partial-equilibrium modules for local market analysis, then connect them through an explicit network or general system model.

## 10. Austrian economics and the knowledge problem

### 10.1 Menger, Mises, Hayek and entrepreneurial discovery

**Core question.** How do actors coordinate when knowledge is dispersed, tacit, contextual and constantly changing?

**Mechanism.** Prices communicate compressed signals; entrepreneurs discover opportunities; competition is a process of experimentation and error correction, not merely a final equilibrium state.

**Representative formal core.** A knowledge-allocation problem can be expressed as:
\[
\max_{a_i\in A_i} \; \mathbb E_i[U_i(a_i)]
\]
where each actor has a local information set \(\mathcal I_i\), not the global information set. The central challenge is that no planner observes all \(\mathcal I_i\) in real time. This is a stylization of the knowledge problem, not an Austrian canonical equation.

**Achievement.** Explains why local knowledge, experimentation, price discovery and decentralized initiative are indispensable.

**Limitations.** Price signals may be distorted by externalities, monopoly, financial bubbles, unequal purchasing power and missing markets. Tacit knowledge is not necessarily reducible to data; decentralized decisions can also create systemic externalities.

**Tension.** Central planning may lack local knowledge; purely decentralized markets may fail to internalize system-wide effects.

**SYNERGY translation.** Use distributed decision-making and open discovery, while building shared standards, public-good provision, risk limits and mechanisms for cross-boundary externalities.

## 11. Keynesian and post-Keynesian traditions

### 11.1 Keynesian macroeconomics

**Core question.** Why can an economy remain below full employment when individual saving and investment plans do not coordinate?

**Representative short-run model.**
\[
Y=C(Y-T)+I(r,\mathbb E)+G+NX.
\]
In a simple closed economy with fixed prices and autonomous investment, the expenditure multiplier is:
\[
k=\frac{1}{1-c}
\]
where \(c\) is the marginal propensity to consume. This result depends on the model's assumptions and leakages; it is not a universal multiplier.

**Achievement.** Explains effective-demand shortfalls, involuntary unemployment, the role of expectations and the potential for stabilizing fiscal and monetary policy.

**Limitations.** Simple multiplier models omit supply constraints, inflation expectations, open-economy leakages, debt sustainability, political implementation and heterogeneous distributional effects. Policy lags and uncertain multipliers matter.

**Tension.** Classical adjustment narratives emphasize price and wage flexibility; Keynesian analysis shows why nominal adjustment may not restore full employment quickly or harmlessly.

**SYNERGY translation.** Track demand, capacity, inventories, employment, liquidity and expectations separately. Design stabilizers at multiple levels, with fiscal and financial limits.

### 11.2 Post-Keynesian and endogenous-money approaches

**Core question.** How do banks create deposits through lending, how do balance sheets and uncertainty shape investment, and why can financial fragility arise endogenously?

**Representative balance-sheet identity.**
\[
\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Equity}.
\]
A bank loan typically creates a corresponding deposit; credit creation is constrained by capital, liquidity, funding, risk, regulation and borrower demand—not simply by a mechanical reserve multiplier.

**Minskyan financial instability.** A stylized classification distinguishes hedge finance (cash flows cover principal and interest), speculative finance (cash flows cover interest but not principal) and Ponzi finance (cash flows do not cover contractual interest without refinancing or asset sales).

**Achievement.** Places credit, leverage, cash-flow commitments and endogenous crises at the centre of macroeconomic analysis.

**Limitations.** Theories differ in operational detail and empirical identification; not every credit expansion is destabilizing, and not every crisis follows one stylized sequence.

**SYNERGY translation.** Separate money issuance, credit creation, settlement, solvency and real wealth. Monitor maturity mismatch, leverage, cash-flow coverage, collateral concentration and network contagion.

## 12. Monetarism, New Classical and New Keynesian macroeconomics

### 12.1 Monetarism

**Core question.** How do money, nominal income, expectations and policy rules influence inflation and economic fluctuations?

**Quantity identity.**
\[
MV=PY.
\]
This is an identity. Treating velocity \(V\) as stable or predictable is an additional empirical assumption, not a mathematical consequence.

**Achievement.** Emphasizes nominal anchors, inflation expectations and the long-run consequences of sustained monetary expansion.

**Limitations.** Financial innovation, endogenous credit, changing velocity and institutional shifts complicate simple money-growth targeting.

**SYNERGY translation.** Track money and credit aggregates alongside prices, output, velocity, balance sheets and payment-system structure; never infer real wealth from nominal issuance.

### 12.2 New Classical and rational-expectations approaches

**Core question.** How do expectations formed using available information affect the real impact of policy and anticipated shocks?

**Representative formal core.**
\[
E_t(\varepsilon_{t+1})=0
\]
for forecast errors under rational expectations, conditional on the model and information set. Rational expectations do not mean perfect foresight.

**Achievement.** Forces policy analysis to account for expectations, credibility and strategic responses to announced rules.

**Limitations.** Representative-agent and common-model assumptions may suppress heterogeneity, learning, bounded rationality, disagreement and institutional constraints. The Lucas critique warns that behavioural relationships may change when policy rules change.

**SYNERGY translation.** Model heterogeneous expectations and strategic adaptation; treat policy rules as endogenous objects that agents learn and respond to.

### 12.3 New Keynesian economics

**Core question.** How do nominal rigidities, imperfect competition and expectations create short-run real effects of monetary policy?

**Representative model core.**
\[
x_t=E_t x_{t+1}-\sigma(i_t-E_t\pi_{t+1}-r_t^n),
\]
\[
\pi_t=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}+\kappa x_t+u_t.
\]
These are stylized IS and Phillips-curve relationships in a common New Keynesian framework; specifications differ across models.

**Achievement.** Combines intertemporal optimization with nominal frictions and explicit policy analysis.

**Limitations.** Results can be sensitive to representative-agent assumptions, friction choices, shock specification and financial-sector modelling. Financial crises and distributional heterogeneity require richer models.

**SYNERGY translation.** Retain expectations and nominal frictions while integrating heterogeneous balance sheets, production networks, credit constraints and institutional diversity.

## 13. Institutional, transaction-cost and property-rights economics

### 13.1 Coase and transaction costs

**Core question.** Why are activities organized inside firms, through markets or through other institutional arrangements?

**Representative make-or-buy condition.**
\[
C_{market}=C_{price}+C_{search}+C_{contract}+C_{monitor}+C_{enforce},
\]
\[
C_{hierarchy}=C_{admin}+C_{agency}+C_{bureaucracy}+C_{adapt}.
\]
An organizational form is attractive when its total coordination cost, including relevant external effects, is lower and it satisfies rights and performance constraints.

**Achievement.** Explains institutional boundaries and why neither markets nor firms are costless.

**Limitations.** Transaction costs are difficult to measure consistently; power, law, distribution and dynamic innovation can be under-specified in simple make-or-buy comparisons.

**SYNERGY translation.** Compare markets, firms, cooperatives, consortia, public agencies and federations as alternative governance technologies for specific tasks.

### 13.2 North and institutional economics

**Core question.** How do formal rules, informal norms, enforcement and path dependence shape incentives and long-run performance?

**Representative reconstruction.**
\[
Y_t=F(K_t,L_t,A_t;\,\mathcal I_t),\qquad
\mathcal I_{t+1}=H(\mathcal I_t,\text{conflict},\text{learning},\text{power}).
\]
Institutions both constrain economic behaviour and evolve through it.

**Achievement.** Explains why similar resources can yield different outcomes under different rules, enforcement and historical paths.

**Limitations.** Institutional quality can be endogenous and hard to measure; broad labels such as “good institutions” risk circular explanation.

**SYNERGY translation.** Specify institutional variables operationally: entry, exit, contract enforcement, appeal, audit, concentration, participation and rule-change costs.

### 13.3 Ostrom and polycentric governance

**Core question.** Under what conditions can communities sustainably govern common-pool resources?

**Mechanisms.** Clearly defined boundaries, locally appropriate rules, collective-choice arrangements, monitoring, graduated sanctions, low-cost conflict resolution, recognition of self-organization and nested governance are among the design principles associated with robust common-pool institutions.

**Formal reconstruction.** A resource stock evolves as:
\[
S_{t+1}=S_t+R(S_t)-H_t,
\]
where \(R(S_t)\) is regeneration and \(H_t\) is extraction. Governance must align extraction and monitoring with resource dynamics, local incentives and external conditions.

**Achievement.** Demonstrates that neither privatization nor centralized state control is the only possible governance solution.

**Limitations.** Local success does not automatically scale; boundaries, power inequalities, ecological change, conflict and cross-level spillovers can undermine institutions. Design principles are not a plug-and-play algorithm.

**SYNERGY translation.** Build nested, overlapping governance with local autonomy, transparent monitoring, appeal, federation-level coordination and explicit limits on what can be decided locally.

## 14. Public choice and constitutional political economy

**Core question.** How do political actors, voters, bureaucracies and organized interests respond to incentives and institutional rules?

**Representative formal core.**
\[
\max_{a\in A} U_j(a;\text{office},\text{budget},\text{ideology},\text{interest groups})
\]
for political actor \(j\), subject to institutional constraints. Public-choice analysis rejects the assumption that public officials automatically maximize a unitary social welfare function.

**Achievement.** Makes rent-seeking, regulatory capture, concentrated benefits, diffuse costs and constitutional constraints part of economic analysis.

**Limitations.** Excessive reduction of public motivation to self-interest can understate professional norms, public service, collective identity and institutional learning.

**SYNERGY translation.** Assume that any concentrated decision authority can be captured—including DAO delegates, validators, AI operators and treasury administrators. Use separation of powers, transparency, rotation where appropriate, independent audit and appeal.

## 15. Schumpeterian and evolutionary economics

### 15.1 Schumpeter

**Core question.** How do entrepreneurs introduce new combinations and transform the structure of production?

**Mechanism.** Innovation disrupts established routines and reallocates resources; credit can finance new combinations, while creative destruction changes market structure.

**Representative growth decomposition.**
\[
\Delta Y=\Delta Y_{capital}+\Delta Y_{labour}+\Delta Y_{technology}+\Delta Y_{reallocation}.
\]
This is an accounting-style decomposition, not a complete Schumpeterian model.

**Achievement.** Places innovation, entrepreneurship, technological discontinuity and structural transformation at the centre of development.

**Limitations.** Innovation can create monopoly power, displacement, regional losses and environmental costs; creative destruction does not guarantee that displaced people can transition successfully.

**SYNERGY translation.** Reward discovery and innovation while designing open standards, contestable markets, transition support and broad participation in innovation rents.

### 15.2 Evolutionary economics and Nelson–Winter

**Core question.** How do firms and institutions evolve through routines, search, selection and learning under bounded rationality?

**Representative formal core.**
\[
x_{i,t+1}=\mathcal R(x_{i,t},\text{search}_{i,t},\text{selection}_{t},\text{environment}_{t}),
\]
where organizational routines evolve rather than being solved from a globally optimized choice problem.

**Achievement.** Explains path dependence, heterogeneous firms, incremental innovation, technological trajectories and non-equilibrium change.

**Limitations.** Selection mechanisms and innovation processes can be difficult to identify; evolutionary success is not synonymous with social desirability.

**SYNERGY translation.** Treat economic institutions as adaptive populations of rules and practices. Evaluate both fitness (survival/productivity) and rights-constrained social outcomes.

## 16. Behavioural economics and bounded rationality

**Core question.** How do actual human decisions differ from idealized optimization under full information and stable preferences?

**Contributions.** Heuristics, framing, loss aversion, present bias, social preferences, reference dependence, limited attention and context-sensitive choice.

**Representative formal core.**
\[
a_i=\arg\max_{a\in A_i}\; \widetilde U_i(a\mid\text{beliefs},\text{attention},\text{frame},\text{social context}),
\]
where \(\widetilde U_i\) need not be stable or fully consistent.

**Achievement.** Improves predictions in domains where psychological regularities materially affect decisions.

**Limitations.** Effects can be context-dependent; findings may vary across populations and environments. Behavioural interventions can become paternalistic or manipulative.

**SYNERGY translation.** Design understandable choices, informed consent, cooling-off and appeal for high-stakes decisions; test interventions and disclose who sets the default.

## 17. Game theory and mechanism design

**Core question.** How do strategic actors behave when outcomes depend on the choices of others, and what rules induce desired outcomes?

**Representative normal-form game.**
\[
u_i(a_i,a_{-i}),\qquad
a_i^*\in\arg\max_{a_i}u_i(a_i,a_{-i}^*).
\]
A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where no player can improve unilaterally. It need not be unique, efficient, fair or stable under learning.

Mechanism design reverses the problem: choose rules so that truthful or socially useful behaviour is incentivized under specified assumptions.

**Achievement.** Formalizes strategic interaction, bargaining, public goods, auctions, coalition formation and incentive compatibility.

**Limitations.** Results depend on information, commitment, equilibrium selection, common knowledge and utility assumptions. Real agents may not compute equilibrium strategies.

**SYNERGY translation.** Test governance and contribution mechanisms against strategic manipulation, collusion, Sybil identities, hidden information and coalition capture—not only cooperative ideal cases.

## 18. Information economics and principal–agent theory

**Core question.** How do adverse selection, moral hazard, signaling and monitoring shape contracts when information is asymmetric?

**Representative principal–agent problem.**
\[
\max_{w(e)}\; \mathbb E[y(e)-w(e)]
\]
subject to the agent's incentive-compatibility and participation constraints:
\[
e\in\arg\max_{e'}\mathbb E[w(e')]-c(e'),\qquad
\mathbb E[w(e)]-c(e)\geq \bar U.
\]

**Achievement.** Explains why contracts, monitoring and compensation must account for hidden action and hidden information.

**Limitations.** Measurable performance can crowd out unmeasured quality, care, cooperation and long-term maintenance. Monitoring can be costly and invasive.

**SYNERGY translation.** Use multiple evidence types, contestable attribution, privacy-preserving verification and safeguards against metric gaming.

## 19. Development economics and structuralism

**Core question.** Why do economies differ in productivity, industrial structure, capabilities and long-run development paths?

**Mechanisms.** Structural transformation, learning-by-doing, coordination failures, human capital, infrastructure, institutions, external constraints and industrial capabilities.

**Representative production-network model.**
\[
x=(I-A)^{-1}d
\]
where \(A\) is the input-coefficient matrix, \(d\) final demand and \(x\) gross output, when the inverse exists and the model's technical-coefficient assumptions hold.

**Achievement.** Shows that development depends on complementary investments and production structures, not simply on aggregate capital accumulation.

**Limitations.** Industrial policy can be captured; infant-industry arguments require evidence of learning spillovers and credible sunset/discipline mechanisms. Development paths are historically contingent.

**SYNERGY translation.** Identify missing complements, bottlenecks and learning spillovers; fund capability-building only with transparent milestones, evaluation and exit rules.

## 20. Ecological economics and environmental economics

### 20.1 Environmental economics

**Core question.** How can external environmental costs and public-good problems be incorporated into economic decisions?

**Representative externality model.**
\[
MSC(q)=MPC(q)+MEC(q),
\]
where marginal social cost equals marginal private cost plus marginal external cost. A Pigouvian tax can align private and social marginal incentives under suitable information and enforcement conditions.

**Achievement.** Provides tools for pollution pricing, cost-benefit analysis, environmental regulation and valuation of external effects.

**Limitations.** Some ecological losses are irreversible, uncertain or not credibly compensable; monetary valuation may not represent rights, thresholds or intrinsic values.

### 20.2 Ecological economics

**Core question.** How is the economy embedded in finite biophysical systems, and how should scale, throughput and distribution be constrained?

**Representative material-balance principle.**
\[
\text{Material input} = \text{useful output} + \text{residuals} + \text{stock change}
\]
under consistent system boundaries.

**Achievement.** Makes energy, materials, entropy, ecological thresholds and intergenerational constraints central.

**Limitations.** Strong sustainability and substitutability assumptions remain debated; operational thresholds and distribution of ecological space require scientific and political judgment.

**SYNERGY translation.** Treat critical ecological constraints as explicit limits, not merely prices to be optimized away. Track physical flows and natural-capital condition separately from financial accounts.

## 21. Welfare economics, capabilities and inequality

### 21.1 Welfare economics

Pareto efficiency is a useful criterion but incomplete as a social objective. Social welfare functions make distributional judgments explicit:
\[
W= W(U_1,\ldots,U_n).
\]
The choice of weights and interpersonal comparisons is normative, not value-neutral.

### 21.2 Sen's capability approach

**Core question.** What real freedoms and capabilities do people have to achieve valued ways of being and doing?

A capability set \(\mathcal C_i\) contains feasible functionings available to person \(i\). Welfare assessment asks not only what a person consumes, but what they are substantively able to do and become.

**Achievement.** Distinguishes resources from the ability to convert resources into real opportunities; accounts for disability, social conditions, public services and agency.

**Limitations.** Capability selection and weighting require public reasoning; measurement is multidimensional and context-dependent.

**SYNERGY translation.** Define a transparent prosperity dashboard with non-substitutable rights and essential floors. Do not reduce human worth to one score.

### 21.3 Inequality and wealth concentration

Piketty's work highlights the dynamics of wealth accumulation and the relationship between returns and growth in particular historical settings. The often-cited comparison \(r>g\) is not a universal law that mechanically predicts inequality; institutions, taxes, saving, inheritance, asset prices, demographics and shocks matter.

**SYNERGY translation.** Measure wealth stocks, income flows, ownership concentration, inheritance, market power and access to productive assets separately.

## 22. Monetary, financial and institutional pluralism

Beyond the major macroeconomic schools, the research programme must study:
- credit unions, mutual credit and cooperative banking;
- public banking and development finance;
- local and complementary currencies;
- payment-versus-payment and delivery-versus-payment settlement;
- clearing houses and netting;
- central-bank money, commercial-bank deposits and e-money;
- asset-backed claims and tokenized rights;
- insolvency, resolution and lender-of-last-resort arrangements.

No monetary instrument should be assessed only by its unit of account or token design. The analysis must identify issuer, redemption promise, legal claim, collateral, maturity, liquidity, settlement finality, loss allocation and insolvency waterfall.

# Part III — Cross-school contradictions and conditional synthesis

## 23. The contradiction matrix

| Tension | School A emphasis | School B emphasis | What is actually in conflict? | SYNERGY research resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Value | Labour/social relations (Marxian) | Preferences/scarcity (marginalist) | Competing exclusive accounts of price/value; different explanatory objects otherwise | Maintain distinct measures: market price, labour time, cost, utility, surplus, capability and external effects |
| Coordination | Price discovery (Hayek) | Macroeconomic stabilization (Keynes) | Local knowledge vs aggregate demand failure only if either is claimed sufficient for all levels | Distributed discovery plus bounded system-level stabilization |
| Planning | Central calculation critique (Austrian) | Public investment/coordination (Keynesian, developmental) | Whether a centre can replace dispersed choice; not whether any shared planning is useful | Distinguish command allocation from public infrastructure, standards, insurance and coordination |
| Ownership | Private property and residual control | Social/collective ownership | Different allocation of control and claims | Plural ownership, explicit rights and comparative institutional tests |
| Competition | Entry and rivalry | Cooperation and common governance | Competition can undermine trust; cooperation can shelter incumbents | Permit cooperation where complementarities exist; preserve contestability and exit |
| Rationality | Optimizing agents | Bounded and socially embedded agents | Full optimization is a benchmark, not a universal psychology | Heterogeneous behavioural models and robustness checks |
| Equilibrium | Clearing and efficiency benchmarks | Disequilibrium, evolution and crisis | Static existence vs dynamic transition and stability | Use equilibrium as one benchmark within a dynamic model |
| Money | Exogenous/controllable aggregates in some models | Endogenous bank credit | Money supply process and policy transmission differ by institutional regime | Model central-bank liabilities, bank deposits, credit, settlement and collateral separately |
| Distribution | Marginal product / market reward | Power, ownership and surplus | Competing explanations of income shares | Decompose income by productivity, scarcity, rents, bargaining, ownership and policy |
| Environment | Substitution and priced externalities | Biophysical limits and critical natural capital | Whether all natural losses can be offset by other capital | Apply thresholds and rights where substitution is unsafe; use prices where appropriate |
| Public authority | Government failure and capture | Market failure and public goods | Both sectors can fail | Comparative institutional analysis, not presumption of virtue |
| Measurement | Single aggregate metrics | Multidimensional welfare | Whether dimensions can be collapsed | Dashboard plus hard constraints and transparent weights |

## 24. Conditional mechanism selection

SYNERGY should not select an institution by ideology. It should compare feasible mechanisms for a defined task.

For a task \(k\), candidate mechanisms \(m\in M_k\) may include spot markets, long-term contracts, firms, cooperatives, consortium cells, public agencies, commons governance and federations.

A first-pass total-cost criterion is:
\[
TC(m,k)=C_{coord}+C_{information}+C_{enforcement}+C_{capital}
+C_{risk}+C_{external}+C_{transition}.
\]

But choosing the lowest measured cost alone is insufficient. A mechanism is admissible only if it satisfies:
\[
Rights(m,k)=1,\quad
Solvency(m,k)=1,\quad
EcologicalLimits(m,k)=1,
\]
and any relevant due-process, competition and security constraints.

Among admissible mechanisms, compare cost, output, distribution, resilience and capability outcomes. The weights are explicit and contestable. Where rights or ecological thresholds are non-substitutable, they are constraints, not terms that can be bought off by higher output.

## 25. The central synthesis hypothesis

The strongest candidate synthesis is not “markets plus planning” as a slogan. It is a **multilevel, polycentric, adaptive institutional system**:

1. Individuals retain agency, consent, exit and rights.
2. Markets and competition discover preferences, prices, technologies and opportunities where exchange is feasible and contestable.
3. Firms, cooperatives and consortia organize repeated, complementary and asset-specific production.
4. Common-resource institutions govern shared assets and local externalities.
5. Public institutions provide legal frameworks, universal guarantees, public goods, systemic insurance and regulation where justified.
6. Federations coordinate cross-boundary infrastructure, standards, risks and spillovers.
7. AI supports forecasting, matching, simulation and audit, but operates under bounded authority and human appeal.
8. Financial institutions provide payments, savings, investment and credit under solvency, liquidity and consumer-protection constraints.
9. Ecological constraints and human rights bound the objective function.

This is a hypothesis about institutional complementarity, not a claim that all layers must be maximized or that every task needs every institution.

# Part IV — Toward the formal theory of SYNERGY

## 26. A multilevel economic state

Let the economy at time \(t\) be represented by:
\[
\mathcal E_t=(V_t,E_t,X_t,\Theta_t,\mathcal I_t,\mathcal B_t,\mathcal N_t)
\]
where:
- \(V_t\): people, households, firms, cooperatives, projects, public bodies and assets;
- \(E_t\): material, financial, contractual, informational, ownership and governance links;
- \(X_t\): measured economic states and balance sheets;
- \(\Theta_t\): technologies, production functions and capabilities;
- \(\mathcal I_t\): institutions, laws, norms and enforcement arrangements;
- \(\mathcal B_t\): behavioural rules, beliefs and expectations;
- \(\mathcal N_t\): natural systems, resource stocks and ecological constraints.

This is a research ontology. Actual data schemas must distinguish observation, contract, estimate, forecast and disputed claim.

## 27. Production, value and wealth

For producer \(i\):
\[
Y_i=F_i(K_i,L_i,H_i,M_i,E_i;\Theta_i)
\]
where produced capital, labour, human capabilities, materials and energy are inputs. The function is context-specific; it may include complementarities, network inputs and ecological limits.

Market revenue is:
\[
R_i=\sum_j p_j q_{ij}.
\]
Accounting profit depends on the accounting boundary and recognized costs. It is not identical to social value, consumer surplus, economic rent or net wealth.

A multidimensional value vector is preferable to a single scalar:
\[
\mathbf V_i=(V_{use},V_{exchange},V_{production},V_{knowledge},
V_{network},V_{social},V_{ecological}).
\]
A scalar score may be constructed for a specific decision only after declaring units, weights, uncertainty and non-substitutable constraints.

Wealth is a stock:
\[
W_t=A_t-L_t,
\]
with assets and liabilities measured under a stated valuation and consolidation policy. Token issuance, internal transfers, gross transaction volume and credit principal do not automatically increase consolidated net wealth.

## 28. Cooperation and net synergy

For a defined group and counterfactual baseline:
\[
S_{net}=\Delta Y_{observed}
-\Delta Y_{explained\ by\ extra\ inputs}
-C_{coord}-C_{integration}-C_{external}.
\]

This equation is a measurement proposal, not a universal natural law. The baseline must be credible; causal identification must address selection, spillovers, time effects and displacement. A positive result in one project does not prove system-wide positive synergy.

At the network level, interaction effects may be represented as:
\[
Y(\mathbf x,G)=\sum_i y_i(x_i)+
\sum_{i<j}\phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j,G)+
\sum_{i<j<k}\phi_{ijk}+\cdots.
\]
Pairwise and higher-order interaction terms can be positive or negative. Coordination, congestion, collusion, contagion and externalities can make network effects harmful.

## 29. Distribution, contribution and ownership

Distribution should be modelled as separate rights and flows:
- current wages and fees;
- contractual profit shares;
- interest and risk compensation;
- dividends and residual claims;
- collective asset benefits;
- access rights to services and infrastructure;
- social guarantees and opportunity support.

A contribution index can support a narrowly defined allocation decision, but must not determine basic rights or human worth. Contribution evidence is multidimensional, context-dependent and contestable. Avoid double counting capital, risk, labour and ownership claims.

A distribution rule should satisfy budget feasibility:
\[
\sum_i d_i + r + i \leq S_{distributable},
\]
where \(d_i\) are distributions, \(r\) reserves and \(i\) reinvestment, subject to prior liabilities and legal claims.

## 30. Money, credit and settlement

The system must distinguish:
1. unit of account;
2. medium of exchange;
3. store of value;
4. issuer liability;
5. credit claim;
6. collateral;
7. payment instruction;
8. settlement asset;
9. finality and discharge of obligation.

For every financial cell, reconcile:
\[
\text{Opening cash}+\text{inflows}-\text{outflows}=\text{closing cash}
\]
and:
\[
\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Equity}.
\]
Inter-cell credit creates claims and obligations; it does not create net consolidated wealth at origination. Risk, maturity, collateral, default and loss allocation must be explicit.

## 31. Prosperity as a constrained vector

Let the prosperity state be:
\[
\mathbf P_t=(p_{income},p_{security},p_{health},p_{education},
p_{capability},p_{ownership},p_{resilience},p_{ecology},p_{agency}).
\]

The objective is not necessarily to maximize a single weighted sum. A candidate formulation is:
\[
\max \; \mathbf P_T
\]
subject to rights floors, essential-service floors, solvency, ecological thresholds, non-discrimination, due process and democratic legitimacy.

If a scalar objective is required for a bounded operational decision, weights must be published and sensitivity-tested. Some constraints must remain hard constraints rather than compensable terms.

# Part V — Proposed constitutional and design commitments

## 32. Status and logical role of the S1–S12 statements

These twelve statements are **not a second independent set of foundational axioms**. Following the reconciliation in [Volume 0A — Axiom Reconciliation and Methodological Audit](VOLUME_0A_AXIOM_RECONCILIATION_AND_METHOD_AUDIT.md), they are retained as proposed constitutional/design commitments and research rules derived from, or constrained by, the canonical A1–A12 foundational set in Volume I. They include normative commitments, operational requirements and methodological rules; they are not all empirical propositions of the same logical kind. The mapping and bridge assumptions are documented in Volume 0A.

### Axiom S1 — Human agency and dignity
A person is not reducible to productivity, creditworthiness, contribution score or economic utility. Basic rights and due process are not conditional on an economic metric.

### Axiom S2 — Real-resource constraint
Every production and distribution system is constrained by real resources, time, capabilities, technology, ecological systems and institutional capacity. Financial claims do not eliminate these constraints.

### Axiom S3 — Plural value
Economic outcomes have multiple dimensions. Price, utility, production, income, wealth, knowledge, capability, social impact and ecological condition are distinct categories.

### Axiom S4 — Distributed knowledge
Relevant economic knowledge is dispersed, incomplete, changing and often tacit. No single actor or model should be presumed to possess it all.

### Axiom S5 — Institutional plurality
Markets, firms, cooperatives, commons, public institutions and federations are alternative and sometimes complementary coordination mechanisms. No one form is universally optimal.

### Axiom S6 — Conditional cooperation
Cooperation can create additional value when complementarities, trust and governance benefits exceed coordination, monitoring, integration and external costs. It can also create collusion and capture.

### Axiom S7 — Contestability and exit
Economic power must be constrained by meaningful entry, exit, appeal, interoperability and protection against coercive dependency.

### Axiom S8 — Explicit rights and obligations
Ownership, control, custody, liability, access and cash-flow rights must be separately defined and evidenced. A ledger entry alone does not create legal or physical truth.

### Axiom S9 — Financial integrity
Money, credit, payment, settlement, liquidity, solvency and real wealth must be separately measured and reconciled. Internal transfers and new liabilities are not automatically net wealth creation.

### Axiom S10 — Reproduction across time
A viable economy must maintain and renew human capabilities, produced assets, institutions and natural systems. Current output cannot be maximized by silently consuming the conditions of future production.

### Axiom S11 — Bounded computational authority
AI and automated systems may recommend or execute delegated actions only within explicit mandates, risk limits, auditability, human appeal and constitutional constraints.

### Axiom S12 — Evidence and falsifiability
Claims of superior performance must be compared with credible baselines, disclose assumptions and uncertainty, and remain open to independent replication and refutation.

## 33. Derived design principles

From the proposed axioms, the project derives these candidate principles:

1. **Mechanism fit:** choose a coordination mechanism for the problem, not for ideological consistency.
2. **Subsidiarity:** decide locally where effects are local; coordinate at higher levels where spillovers, scale or shared infrastructure require it.
3. **Plural ownership:** allow personal, enterprise, cooperative, public and common ownership with explicit rights.
4. **Open discovery:** preserve competitive entry, experimentation and entrepreneurial initiative.
5. **Cooperative surplus:** measure and share demonstrable joint gains without hiding costs or risks.
6. **Contribution without human ranking:** reward verified contribution in context while protecting unconditional rights.
7. **Resilience:** account for reserves, redundancy, concentration, liquidity and recovery, not just average efficiency.
8. **Ecological ceilings:** impose physical limits where thresholds or irreversibility make monetary substitution unsafe.
9. **Privacy by design:** disclose the minimum data needed for verification and governance.
10. **Constitutional AI:** separate recommendation, authorization, execution and audit.
11. **Anti-capture:** constrain concentrated power in markets, states, cooperatives, DAOs and AI infrastructure alike.
12. **Experimental governance:** test rules, publish outcomes, permit appeal and revise under transparent procedures.

# Part VI — Falsifiable propositions and research design

## 34. Initial hypotheses

Each hypothesis is provisional. It requires a defined population, outcome, comparison group, time horizon and identification strategy.

**H1 — Conditional coordination advantage.** For repeated activities with high asset specificity and measurable interdependence, a well-governed consortium may reduce total coordination cost relative to repeated spot contracting. Test against firm, contract and cooperative alternatives.

**H2 — Local knowledge advantage.** Decentralized decision rights improve adaptation when local information is important and spillovers are limited. The effect should weaken when decisions create large cross-cell externalities.

**H3 — Polycentric resilience.** A network of semi-autonomous units with diversified dependencies may recover better from localized shocks than an equally concentrated system, provided interconnection does not create excessive contagion.

**H4 — Cooperation is non-monotonic.** Increasing cooperation initially may create complementarities, but beyond some governance or concentration threshold, coordination cost, collusion and capture may dominate.

**H5 — Broad ownership and investment incentives.** Access to productive ownership can improve long-run participation and asset maintenance when rights are enforceable and governance costs are controlled. It may fail where capital is thin, members are heterogeneous or liquidity needs are high.

**H6 — Contribution metrics are vulnerable to gaming.** A single contribution score will induce substitution toward measured activities and neglect unmeasured contributions more than a plural, contestable assessment with qualitative review.

**H7 — Digital coordination has conditional benefits.** Digital twins and automated matching reduce search and reconciliation costs where source data are accurate, interoperable and consented; benefits disappear or reverse with poor data, surveillance or platform lock-in.

**H8 — Credit network contagion.** Inter-cell credit can improve capital allocation but increases correlated default risk as exposure concentration and maturity mismatch rise, absent capital, liquidity and resolution safeguards.

**H9 — Shared reserves improve shock absorption.** Pooled reserves can reduce local insolvency risk when contributions, withdrawal rules and loss waterfalls are credible; they can also create moral hazard and correlated runs.

**H10 — AI coordination is bounded by model uncertainty.** AI-assisted resource matching improves specified operational outcomes only where data coverage, feedback quality and objective alignment are sufficient; human review and appeal reduce some classes of harmful error.

**H11 — Competition plus cooperation.** Cooperative production combined with open external competition can outperform protected consortia on innovation and cost when entry and interoperability are maintained.

**H12 — Capability floors change measured success.** Systems assessed on capability and security floors may rank differently from systems ranked solely by GDP, profit or asset value.

**H13 — Ecological constraints change optimal allocation.** Where resource thresholds are binding or losses irreversible, unconstrained price optimization can recommend socially unacceptable paths.

**H14 — Network effects can be positive or negative.** Increasing connectivity raises the potential for matching and knowledge spillovers but also for congestion, systemic risk, collusion and contagion. The net effect depends on topology and governance.

**H15 — Institutional diversity improves robustness under heterogeneity.** A portfolio of governance mechanisms may outperform a uniform institutional design when tasks and local conditions differ, provided interfaces and rights are compatible.

## 35. Required baselines

No SYNERGY experiment should compare itself only with an intentionally weak baseline. At minimum, test against:
1. decentralized spot markets;
2. long-term bilateral contracts;
3. vertically integrated firms;
4. investor-owned platform coordination;
5. worker or producer cooperatives;
6. public or municipal provision where relevant;
7. common-pool governance;
8. centralized planning or optimization as a computational benchmark;
9. hybrid networks without AI;
10. the same hybrid network with AI support.

Use matched resource endowments, technology, demand, information and shock exposure wherever possible. Report both average outcomes and distributional tails.

## 36. Metrics and causal identification

Measure separately:
- output, productivity and quality;
- consumer and producer surplus where estimable;
- total coordination and transaction costs;
- income, wealth and productive-asset ownership distribution;
- access to opportunity and essential services;
- liquidity, solvency, leverage and default;
- resilience, recovery time and correlated failure;
- market concentration and entry;
- trust, participation and governance cost;
- material throughput, emissions and natural-asset condition;
- privacy incidents, appeal rates and algorithmic error.

Use randomized experiments when ethical and feasible; otherwise use credible quasi-experimental methods, matched comparisons, difference-in-differences, instrumental variables where defensible, synthetic controls, process tracing and agent-based calibration. Pre-register hypotheses and primary outcomes when possible. Publish null and negative results.

## 37. Simulation discipline

An agent-based or network simulation must specify:
- agent types and heterogeneous endowments;
- decision rules and information sets;
- production and consumption technologies;
- price formation and market-clearing rules;
- contracts, ownership and default;
- institutional rules and enforcement;
- network topology and edge capacities;
- shocks and random processes;
- initialization and calibration;
- measurement and aggregation;
- sensitivity analysis and ablation tests.

A cellular automaton is a computational representation of local transition rules, not an economic theory by itself. A simulation that assumes cooperative agents, perfect verification or costless trust cannot then claim to have demonstrated that cooperation, verification or trust will arise in practice.

## 38. Failure and refutation conditions

The SYNERGY research programme must be willing to revise or reject its claims. Examples:
- consortium governance persistently costs more than feasible alternatives for the targeted tasks;
- broad ownership reduces investment or maintenance without offsetting benefits;
- digital coordination increases concentration, surveillance or exclusion more than it reduces transaction costs;
- inter-cell finance produces unacceptable contagion even under conservative limits;
- contribution systems systematically discriminate or become unappealable;
- local autonomy worsens cross-boundary externalities that federations cannot control;
- claimed synergy disappears after controlling for additional inputs, selection and displacement;
- prosperity metrics can be gamed without reliable correction.

A theory that cannot specify what evidence would count against it is not yet a scientific research programme.

## 41. Concluding proposition

The proposed scientific contribution of SYNERGISTIC ECONOMICS is not the claim that markets, states, cooperatives, AI or blockchain are individually sufficient. It is the hypothesis that **economic performance and broadly distributed prosperity depend on the conditional interaction of multiple coordination mechanisms under explicit rights, financial, ecological and evidentiary constraints**.

The theory succeeds only if it can do more than restate pluralism. It must identify the conditions under which each mechanism works, predict the consequences of combining them, measure the costs of interaction, and outperform credible alternatives in reproducible tests.

That is the standard by which this research programme should be judged.
