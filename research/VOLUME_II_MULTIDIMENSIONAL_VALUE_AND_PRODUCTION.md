## Abstract

This volume develops the next theoretical layer of SYNERGISTIC ECONOMICS: a multidimensional account of economic value and a production theory capable of representing individual activity, organizations, networks, collective assets, knowledge, finance and ecological constraints without collapsing them into one monetary number.

The central distinction is that **price, utility, production, value added, surplus, profit, income, wealth, capability, social impact and ecological condition are different analytical objects**. They can be related, but they are not interchangeable. A rigorous theory must identify the question each measure answers, the system boundary within which it is calculated, its unit, time horizon, valuation convention and uncertainty.

The volume proposes:
- a taxonomy of value concepts and their relations;
- a multi-input, multi-output production framework;
- a distinction between productive capital services and asset wealth;
- an account of complementarity, specialization, learning and network effects;
- a counterfactual method for measuring net synergy;
- a separation of value creation, value capture and value distribution;
- a multidimensional prosperity framework with non-substitutable rights and ecological constraints;
- falsifiable hypotheses and a measurement programme.

The framework is a research proposal, not a new universally accepted theory of value. It does not claim to solve all disputes between classical, Marxian, marginalist, institutional, ecological and capability approaches by definition. It preserves distinct concepts where they answer different questions and identifies the conditions under which they can be connected.

# Part I — The problem of value

## 1. Why one theory of value is not enough for the whole economy

Economic thought has used the word “value” for several distinct purposes:

1. **Exchange valuation:** what price is paid or expected in a particular exchange?
2. **Subjective valuation:** how does an actor rank alternatives under scarcity and constraints?
3. **Production accounting:** what output and value added are generated during a period?
4. **Classical and Marxian value analysis:** how are production, labour, surplus and social relations organized?
5. **Welfare analysis:** how do allocations affect consumer and producer surplus or social welfare?
6. **Capability analysis:** what real opportunities and functionings are available to people?
7. **Ecological valuation:** what natural assets and biophysical processes sustain life and production?
8. **Network and innovation analysis:** what additional effects arise from interaction, knowledge diffusion, standards and shared infrastructure?

These are not merely rival labels for one hidden quantity. Some are measurement conventions, some are theoretical constructs, some are normative evaluations, and some are causal mechanisms.

A market price can rise because scarcity increases, market power expands, expected future income changes or speculative demand grows. None of these events alone proves that physical output, human capability or social welfare has increased.

An activity can produce large social benefits without generating an equivalent cash flow. Conversely, an activity can generate substantial private revenue while imposing costs on third parties or depleting natural assets.

The research task is therefore not to find one number called “true value”. It is to build a consistent system of distinct measures and specify how they relate.

## 2. The basic category map

| Concept | Type | Main question | Typical unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preference / utility | Behavioural-theoretical | How does an actor rank alternatives? | Ordinal index or model-specific utility |
| Price | Exchange observation | At what terms does exchange occur? | Currency per unit |
| Output | Production flow | What goods/services were produced? | Physical units or constant-price volume |
| Intermediate consumption | Production flow | What inputs were used up in production? | Currency or physical quantities |
| Value added | Production-accounting flow | What value was added by a producer or sector? | Currency per period |
| Surplus | Theory-dependent residual | What remains after specified claims/costs? | Defined by the theory |
| Profit | Accounting/economic result | What remains after recognized costs? | Currency per period |
| Income | Distribution flow | What resources accrue to a person or institution? | Currency per period |
| Wealth | Balance-sheet stock | What is the value of assets net of liabilities? | Currency at a date |
| Capital services | Productive input flow | What productive services do assets provide? | Service quantity or rental-price-weighted index |
| Capability | Human opportunity state | What can a person actually do or become? | Multidimensional indicators |
| Ecological condition | Biophysical stock/flow | What is the state of ecosystems and resource stocks? | Physical indicators and carefully defined valuations |
| Net synergy | Counterfactual causal effect | What additional result is caused by interaction? | Outcome-specific units, often a vector |

The unit is not a cosmetic detail. Adding a physical output, a monetary flow, an ordinal utility index and an ecological threshold into one equation without a defensible conversion is a dimensional error.

## 3. Value statements require a declared boundary

Every value claim must state:

- **Subject:** person, household, firm, consortium, sector, region, state, global system or ecosystem.
- **Object:** product, service, asset, activity, relationship, institution or outcome.
- **Boundary:** which entities and transactions are included; whether accounts are consolidated.
- **Time:** instant, accounting period, lifecycle or intergenerational horizon.
- **Unit:** physical, monetary, index, ordinal ranking or vector.
- **Valuation basis:** market price, replacement cost, opportunity cost, present value, shadow price, administered price or non-monetary indicator.
- **Counterfactual:** what would have happened without the activity or intervention.
- **Uncertainty:** data coverage, model uncertainty, confidence interval and sensitivity.

A statement such as “the consortium created $10 million of value” is incomplete until it specifies whether $10 million means revenue, value added, profit, asset appreciation, avoided cost, social benefit or a counterfactual net effect.

# Part II — Theories of value: comparison and integration

## 4. Classical political economy: production, distribution and surplus

Classical political economy examined the conditions of production, the division of the social product and the distribution of income among wages, profits and rents. It did not use one uniform theory across all authors.

A simplified social product account may be written:
\[
Q = C_{int}+VA,
\]
where gross output \(Q\) is divided into intermediate consumption \(C_{int}\) and value added \(VA\), under a consistent accounting boundary.

The classical concern with surplus asks how the part of the social product beyond specified replacement and subsistence requirements is generated and distributed. The precise definition differs across Smith, Ricardo, Marx and later classical interpretations.

**What this tradition contributes to SYNERGY**
- production must be distinguished from circulation and exchange;
- distribution is part of the economic system, not an afterthought;
- rent, bargaining power and ownership can affect who receives the surplus;
- accumulation and reproduction determine future productive capacity.

**What must not be conflated**
- classical surplus is not automatically identical to accounting profit;
- market price is not a direct measurement of socially necessary labour time;
- value added in national accounts is not a direct empirical validation of any one classical theory of value.

## 5. Marxian value analysis

Marxian analysis distinguishes use-value, exchange-value, value, concrete and abstract labour, socially necessary labour time, surplus value and the circulation of capital. These are interrelated theoretical categories.

In a simplified notation:
\[
c+v+s,
\]
where \(c\) is constant capital advanced, \(v\) variable capital advanced and \(s\) surplus value in the theory's conceptual system. The rate of surplus value is:
\[
e=\frac{s}{v}.
\]

These equations are not national-accounting identities and should not be used as if they were observed accounting line items. The relation between Marxian value categories, production prices and observed market prices is a substantive theoretical dispute.

**Contribution to SYNERGY**
- analyse control of productive assets and the distribution of residual claims;
- examine how labour, technology, ownership and bargaining relations shape production;
- distinguish creation of a product from appropriation of its income;
- investigate accumulation and concentration over time.

**Limitations and open questions**
- the labour theory of value and transformation problem remain contested;
- the operational measurement of abstract labour and socially necessary labour time is difficult;
- a theory of surplus appropriation does not by itself fully specify consumer preference, ecological thresholds, institutional variety or entrepreneurial discovery.

SYNERGY therefore preserves Marxian categories as a distinct analytical lens rather than translating them into marginal utility or accounting profit.

## 6. Marginalist and neoclassical value

Marginalist theory explains choice and relative valuation through preferences, scarcity and constraints.

A consumer problem can be represented:
\[
\max_{x\geq0} U(x)\quad\text{subject to}\quad p\cdot x\leq m.
\]
For an interior optimum:
\[
\frac{MU_i}{MU_j}=\frac{p_i}{p_j}.
\]

This framework is powerful for analysing substitution, demand, willingness to pay and marginal trade-offs. But willingness to pay depends on income and endowments; it is not a complete measure of need, rights or human importance.

**Contribution to SYNERGY**
- model demand, scarcity and opportunity costs;
- estimate how choices respond to prices and constraints;
- analyse consumer and producer surplus under clearly stated assumptions.

**Limitations**
- utility is often ordinal and not directly comparable between people;
- market demand weights preferences by purchasing power;
- externalities, market power, missing markets and unequal endowments can make market prices diverge from social opportunity costs.

## 7. Institutional and transaction-cost value

Institutional economics highlights that value depends partly on the rules and organizational arrangements through which people coordinate.

A task can be organized by a spot market, repeated contract, firm, cooperative, consortium, public agency or commons institution. Each arrangement has costs:
\[
TC_m=C_{search}+C_{bargain}+C_{contract}+C_{monitor}+C_{enforce}
+C_{adapt}+C_{governance}.
\]

The least-cost mechanism is not automatically the most legitimate or socially desirable. Rights, power, external effects and resilience remain constraints.

**Contribution to SYNERGY:** treat governance quality, trust, interoperability and conflict resolution as measurable institutional conditions, while avoiding the claim that they can always be converted into one monetary value.

## 8. Capability and welfare value

The capability approach evaluates real opportunities rather than only resources or subjective satisfaction.

Let person \(i\) have resources \(r_i\), conversion conditions \(z_i\), and feasible capability set:
\[
\mathcal C_i=\Phi(r_i,z_i).
\]
Two people with identical income may have different feasible opportunities because of health, disability, geography, care responsibilities, safety, education or access to infrastructure.

**Contribution to SYNERGY**
- evaluate whether productive and social systems expand meaningful choice;
- distinguish resource provision from the ability to use resources;
- protect basic rights and essential capability floors.

**Limitation:** capability selection and weighting require public reasoning; there is no universally agreed single capability index.

## 9. Ecological and biophysical value

Economic activity is embedded in physical systems. Production uses energy and materials and generates residuals. Some ecosystem functions are difficult or impossible to replace.

A physical balance for material \(m\) can be represented:
\[
I_m=O_m+R_m+\Delta S_m,
\]
where inputs \(I_m\) are allocated to useful outputs \(O_m\), residuals \(R_m\), and changes in stocks \(\Delta S_m\), within a declared system boundary.

Monetary environmental valuation can inform decisions, but a monetary price does not guarantee that an ecological threshold is safe. Where losses are irreversible or critical thresholds are uncertain, physical constraints may be more appropriate than a compensating price.

**Contribution to SYNERGY:** maintain physical resource accounts and critical ecological limits alongside financial and production accounts.

# Part III — A multidimensional value architecture

## 10. The SYNERGY value vector

For an economic activity \(a\), define a vector:
\[
\mathbf V(a)=
\begin{bmatrix}
V_{use}(a)\\
V_{exchange}(a)\\
V_{production}(a)\\
V_{distribution}(a)\\
V_{capability}(a)\\
V_{network}(a)\\
V_{ecological}(a)
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The dimensions mean:

1. **Use value:** usefulness to a specified user or purpose.
2. **Exchange value:** observed or contractually agreed exchange terms.
3. **Production value:** contribution to measured output and value added.
4. **Distribution value:** incidence of income, ownership, risk and claims among affected parties.
5. **Capability value:** effect on real opportunities, agency and essential capabilities.
6. **Network value:** incremental effects arising from connections, complementarity, standards, shared infrastructure and knowledge diffusion.
7. **Ecological value:** effects on natural stocks, ecosystem functions, material throughput and environmental thresholds.

This is a vector, not a claim that all components share one unit. Each component requires its own operational definition and data.

A particular decision may use a weighted index:
\[
V_w(a)=\sum_k w_k\,\tilde V_k(a),
\]
but only after normalizing dimensions, publishing weights, testing sensitivity and preserving non-substitutable constraints. The weighted score must never hide a rights violation or ecological threshold breach.

## 11. Value creation, value capture and value distribution

Three questions must be separated.

### 11.1 Creation

What additional output, service, knowledge, capability or avoided cost is produced?

### 11.2 Capture

Which actors can convert some of the created result into revenue, rent, profit, intellectual-property income or other private claims?

### 11.3 Distribution

Who receives wages, fees, profits, dividends, ownership, access, risk exposure and social benefits?

A platform may create matching efficiency while capturing much of the surplus through fees or data control. A cooperative may distribute more of the surplus to members but incur higher governance costs. A public infrastructure may generate broad benefits that do not appear as a direct cash flow to its operator.

SYNERGY must measure all three stages. A rise in total value creation does not imply a fair distribution; a change in distribution does not automatically imply a change in total production.

# Part IV — A general theory of production

## 12. Production as constrained transformation

Production is the organized transformation of inputs into outputs over time, using technology, knowledge, institutions and natural resources.

For producer \(i\):
\[
\mathbf y_i(t)=F_i(\mathbf x_i(t),\mathbf k_i(t),\mathbf h_i(t),
\mathbf n_i(t),\theta_i(t),\mathcal I_i(t)),
\]
where:
- \(\mathbf y_i\): vector of outputs and by-products;
- \(\mathbf x_i\): intermediate materials, energy and services;
- \(\mathbf k_i\): productive asset services;
- \(\mathbf h_i\): labour and human capabilities;
- \(\mathbf n_i\): natural inputs and ecosystem services;
- \(\theta_i\): technology and organizational knowledge;
- \(\mathcal I_i\): institutional and contractual conditions.

The function is not assumed to be known, smooth, convex or constant. It may include thresholds, complementarities, learning, congestion and irreversibility.

## 13. Production functions: what the standard forms can and cannot do

### 13.1 Cobb–Douglas

\[
Y=A K^\alpha L^\beta.
\]

Useful for a tractable benchmark and growth accounting. The elasticities are constant under the model. The form can impose restrictive substitution and aggregation assumptions and does not automatically represent bottlenecks, ecological thresholds or heterogeneous technologies.

### 13.2 Constant elasticity of substitution (CES)

\[
Y=A\left[\delta K^\rho+(1-\delta)L^\rho\right]^{1/\rho},
\qquad \sigma=\frac{1}{1-\rho}.
\]

Allows a constant elasticity of substitution between inputs. It remains a stylized representation and does not imply that every input can be substituted at every scale.

### 13.3 Leontief / fixed proportions

\[
Y=\min\left(\frac{K}{a},\frac{L}{b},\frac{E}{c}\right).
\]

Useful for bottleneck and complementary-input settings. It can represent cases where an output is limited by the scarcest required input. It is not a universal description of production.

### 13.4 Translog

A flexible second-order approximation:
\[
\ln Y=\beta_0+\sum_i\beta_i\ln x_i+
\frac12\sum_i\sum_j\beta_{ij}\ln x_i\ln x_j.
\]

It allows more flexible substitution patterns but requires sufficient data, careful estimation and attention to regularity conditions.

### 13.5 Network production

In an input-output representation:
\[
\mathbf x=(I-A)^{-1}\mathbf d,
\]
where \(A\) is the matrix of input coefficients, \(\mathbf d\) final demand and \(\mathbf x\) gross output, provided the inverse exists and the technical-coefficient assumptions hold.

This captures interindustry dependencies, but a fixed-coefficient input-output model does not by itself model price adjustment, innovation, substitution, strategic behaviour or changing production technology.

**SYNERGY rule:** no single production function is treated as the universal production law. Select and test a functional form for the specific sector, scale and question; compare results under alternative forms.

## 14. Inputs and productive services

A complete production model should distinguish at least:

- labour hours and task composition;
- skill, experience and learning;
- produced capital stock;
- capital services delivered by assets;
- materials and intermediate inputs;
- energy and water;
- land and ecosystem services;
- software, data and intellectual property;
- management, organization and coordination;
- infrastructure and network access;
- institutional reliability and contract enforcement.

The distinction between a capital asset's market value and the productive service it provides is essential. A machine's resale value is a wealth measure; its capacity, utilization, reliability and productive contribution are service measures.

## 15. Capital stock, capital services and depreciation

Let the productive capital stock of asset class \(j\) evolve:
\[
K_{j,t+1}=(1-\delta_j)K_{j,t}+I_{j,t},
\]
where \(I_{j,t}\) is investment and \(\delta_j\) is depreciation or retirement under the selected model.

Capital services are a flow drawn from the productive stock:
\[
CS_{j,t}=u_{j,t}\,K^{prod}_{j,t},
\]
where \(u_{j,t}\) captures utilization or service intensity in a simplified formulation.

Capital services must not be confused with net wealth. Productive capital stocks are adjusted for efficiency loss and retirement; wealth stocks are valued assets net of liabilities. A taxi's productive service is its transport capacity, reliability and trips; its market resale price is a different measure.

## 16. Labour, human capital and care

Labour is not homogeneous. A model should distinguish hours, task, skill, experience, working conditions, occupational risk and output quality. Human capital is a theoretical and statistical construct; it is not ownership of a person or a claim over their future labour.

Care, household production and unpaid activity may be essential to social reproduction while being incompletely represented in market output. The treatment of non-market production depends on the accounting framework and measurement purpose.

SYNERGY must not convert a person's worth into a wage, productivity estimate or contribution score. Productivity measurement is for a defined production question, not a measure of human dignity.

## 17. Knowledge, data and technology

Knowledge can be:
- tacit or codified;
- private, licensed, open or common;
- embodied in people, routines, software and equipment;
- cumulative and path-dependent;
- non-rival in use but costly to produce, maintain, verify and govern.

Data may be a produced asset in some accounting treatments, but not every data point is an economically controlled asset. Data quality, lawful rights, consent, provenance, maintenance, interoperability and exclusion costs matter.

Technology affects production through equipment, software, organizational routines, standards and learning. A residual productivity estimate should not automatically be called “technology”; it may include measurement error, utilization, composition, management, scale, spillovers and market structure.

## 18. Organization and coordination as production conditions

Production often requires coordination across complementary inputs. Let a project require input vector \(x\), and let coordination quality be \(q_c\):
\[
Y=F(x,q_c,\theta).
\]

The coordination mechanism may change:
- search and matching costs;
- delays and idle capacity;
- contract and monitoring costs;
- information loss;
- quality assurance;
- risk pooling;
- learning and adaptation;
- bargaining power and rent capture.

Coordination is not automatically productive. It may also generate bureaucracy, surveillance, lock-in, collusion and additional overhead. Its net contribution must be measured.

# Part V — Productivity and growth accounting

## 19. Labour productivity

\[
LP=\frac{Y}{H},
\]
where \(Y\) is a specified output measure and \(H\) hours worked. Interpretation depends on output quality, sector composition, outsourcing, capital deepening and measurement boundaries.

## 20. Multifactor productivity

A simplified growth-accounting relationship:
\[
\Delta\ln Y\approx
s_K\Delta\ln K+
s_L\Delta\ln L+
\Delta\ln A,
\]
where input shares \(s_K,s_L\) weight capital and labour growth, and \(A\) is multifactor productivity under the chosen framework.

The residual \(\Delta\ln A\) is not a pure observation of innovation or technology. It can include organizational change, knowledge, network effects, scale, adjustment costs, imperfect competition, input-quality changes and measurement errors.

## 21. Productivity is not the same as prosperity

Productivity can rise while:
- employment falls or becomes more precarious;
- market power increases;
- output quality declines;
- ecological costs rise;
- ownership becomes more concentrated;
- unpaid work or household burdens increase;
- the benefits accrue to a narrow group.

Therefore productivity is an important explanatory variable and policy indicator, but it is not a complete welfare objective.

# Part VI — Cooperation, complementarity and economic synergy

## 22. Complementarity

Two inputs are complementary when the marginal contribution of one increases with the amount of the other. For differentiable production function \(F(x_1,x_2)\), a local complementarity condition is:
\[
\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial x_1\partial x_2}>0.
\]

This condition is local and depends on the variables and units chosen. It does not prove that a specific organization or network is beneficial; governance and coordination costs must still be counted.

Examples of potential complementarity:
- skilled workers and specialized equipment;
- data and trained analysts;
- production capacity and logistics;
- renewable generation and storage;
- research knowledge and manufacturing capability;
- shared standards and interoperable systems.

## 23. Network effects

A network may increase the usefulness of a service as participants or compatible nodes increase. But network effects can be:
- direct or indirect;
- positive or negative;
- local or system-wide;
- linear, nonlinear or threshold-dependent;
- reversible or path-dependent.

Connectivity can improve matching and learning, but can also increase congestion, systemic contagion, lock-in, surveillance and collusion.

A generic network output model is:
\[
Y(\mathbf x,G)=\sum_i f_i(x_i)+
\sum_{i<j}\phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j,G)+
\sum_{i<j<k}\phi_{ijk}+\cdots.
\]

The interaction terms \(\phi\) may be positive or negative. Their empirical identification is difficult because connected actors may select into networks and because spillovers contaminate control groups.

## 24. The counterfactual definition of net synergy

For a defined intervention or collaboration \(C\), define:
\[
S_{net}(C)=
\Delta Y_{observed}
-\Delta Y_{extra\ inputs}
-C_{coord}
-C_{integration}
-C_{external}
-C_{risk}.
\]

Every term must use compatible units or be reported as a vector. If the outcome is multi-dimensional, the primary result should be a vector:
\[
\mathbf S_{net}=(S_{output},S_{quality},S_{capability},
S_{resilience},S_{ecology},S_{distribution}).
\]

A monetary scalar may be estimated for a narrowly defined analysis only if valuation assumptions are explicit and the result is accompanied by non-monetary indicators and hard constraints.

### 24.1 Counterfactual baseline

The relevant baseline is not “zero activity”. It is the best credible alternative without the collaboration:
- independent production;
- ordinary market contracting;
- existing firm hierarchy;
- existing cooperative;
- public provision;
- another network design.

A comparison with an artificially weak baseline exaggerates synergy.

### 24.2 Additional inputs

Control for:
- additional labour and hours;
- additional capital and equipment;
- additional energy and materials;
- subsidies and tax advantages;
- risk transfer to third parties;
- unpaid labour;
- data and infrastructure supplied by others;
- displacement of activity elsewhere.

### 24.3 Coordination and integration costs

Include governance, meetings, software, integration, legal work, auditing, monitoring, dispute resolution, security, training, switching, delay and maintenance.

### 24.4 External and systemic costs

Include pollution, congestion, concentration, correlated credit exposure, privacy loss, exclusion and risks shifted outside the measured organization.

### 24.5 Causal identification

Possible methods include randomized pilots where ethical and feasible; matched comparisons; difference-in-differences; synthetic controls; instrumental variables where defensible; regression discontinuity; event studies; process tracing; and calibrated agent-based models.

No method automatically solves selection, spillover or external-validity problems. The design and assumptions must be published.

## 25. Why “more transactions” is not synergy

Transaction count, gross merchandise value, token transfers, TVL, membership and network size are activity measures. They may be useful operational indicators but are not proof of additional output or welfare.

For example:
- a transfer of $100 between two accounts inside one consolidated group does not create $100 of group revenue;
- a $100 loan creates a financial asset and liability, not $100 of net consolidated wealth;
- a token price increase can change market capitalization without increasing productive capacity;
- adding users to a network can raise congestion or fraud costs as well as potential matching value.

A synergy claim must identify the outcome and counterfactual, not simply count activity.

# Part VII — Distribution, ownership and value capture

## 26. From production to claims

A production process creates outputs and obligations. Distribution determines who receives the resulting flows and rights.

For an organization, the distributable residual is not simply gross revenue:
\[
S_{available}=R-C_{intermediate}-C_{labour}-C_{maintenance}
-C_{tax}-C_{debt}-C_{contractual}-C_{risk\ provisions},
\]
subject to the accounting and legal framework. The expression is schematic: some costs are already included in other categories and must not be double-counted.

Distribution rules should specify:
- wages and fees;
- supplier payments;
- interest and risk compensation;
- taxes and public obligations;
- reinvestment and maintenance;
- reserves and insurance;
- dividends and residual claims;
- collective asset contributions;
- social and ecological remediation.

## 27. Ownership is a bundle of rights

Separate:
- legal title;
- beneficial ownership;
- custody;
- use and access;
- operational control;
- governance rights;
- income rights;
- residual claims;
- transfer rights;
- liability and encumbrances.

An individual may own an asset but lease it to a consortium; a consortium may operate infrastructure without owning the land; an investor may hold a contractual cash-flow claim without controlling operations. Digital representation must preserve these distinctions.

## 28. The production-distribution feedback loop

Distribution affects future production through health, skills, investment, incentives, demand, risk-taking, trust and ownership. Production also affects future distribution through wages, profits, rents, asset prices and bargaining power.

A dynamic model should therefore connect:
\[
X_{t+1}=F(X_t,\,production_t,\,distribution_t,\,institutions_t,\,shocks_t).
\]

Distribution is not merely a final division of a fixed pie. It can change the size, composition, quality and resilience of future production. But redistributive mechanisms can also create administrative costs or incentive effects. Both directions must be tested.

# Part VIII — Prosperity and sustainability

## 29. A prosperity vector

Let the prosperity state be:
\[
\mathbf P_t=
(P_{income},P_{security},P_{health},P_{education},
P_{agency},P_{capability},P_{ownership},P_{resilience},P_{ecology}).
\]

Each dimension needs a defined indicator, population coverage, time horizon and data source. Some dimensions can be monetized for a specific decision; others should remain physical, institutional or rights-based indicators.

## 30. Hard constraints and weighted objectives

A naive optimization:
\[
\max \sum_k w_k P_k
\]
can allow a large gain in one dimension to compensate mathematically for a severe rights violation or irreversible ecological loss.

A safer structure is:
\[
\max \; \mathbf P_T
\]
subject to:
\[
Rights_i\geq R_{min},\quad
Essential_i\geq E_{min},\quad
Ecology_j\in\mathcal E_{safe},\quad
Solvency\geq S_{min}.
\]

The constraints and thresholds require legitimate legal, scientific and democratic processes. The model must disclose uncertainty and avoid pretending that disputed ethical choices are technical facts.

## 31. Wealth accounting and intergenerational continuity

A balance-sheet view complements production-flow measures:
\[
W_t=A^{produced}_t+A^{human}_t+A^{natural}_t+A^{net\ foreign}_t-L_t,
\]
subject to the chosen wealth-accounting framework and valuation rules.

This expression is conceptual. In practice, asset coverage, valuation, discount rates, liabilities, ecosystem thresholds and substitutability assumptions vary.

A minimum sustainability question is whether real comprehensive wealth per capita is maintained, but this criterion alone is not sufficient where critical natural assets have limited substitutes or rights and distribution are ignored.

## 32. Strong and weak substitutability

A weak-sustainability approach allows losses in one capital category to be offset by gains in another, under specified valuation assumptions. Strong-sustainability approaches identify critical natural assets or ecological functions that cannot safely be substituted.

SYNERGY should not assume universal substitutability. For each natural asset, identify:
- whether it is renewable;
- regeneration rate;
- threshold and irreversibility risk;
- substitutes and their limits;
- distribution of access and damage;
- uncertainty and monitoring quality.

# Part IX — Measurement architecture for SYNERGY

## 33. A layered measurement system

| Layer | Core indicators | What it must not be confused with |
|---|---|---|
| Physical production | Units, quality, capacity, utilization, waste | Revenue or token volume |
| National / sector accounts | Output, intermediate consumption, value added, income | Comprehensive welfare |
| Organization accounts | Revenue, cost, profit, assets, liabilities, cash flow | Social value |
| Productivity | Output per labour hour; capital services; MFP | Pure technology or human worth |
| Ownership and distribution | Asset shares, income shares, control, claims, risks | Equal opportunity or capability by itself |
| Network and synergy | Incremental matching, delay, cost, quality and spillover effects | Network size or transaction count |
| Human prosperity | Capabilities, security, health, education, agency | Single income metric |
| Ecological condition | Physical stocks, flows, thresholds, ecosystem services | Fully substitutable monetary value |

## 34. Data quality and provenance

Every observation should include:
- source and responsible reporting entity;
- timestamp and validity period;
- unit and currency;
- scope and consolidation boundary;
- observed / estimated / forecast / contracted / disputed status;
- methodology and transformations;
- uncertainty and revision history;
- privacy and permission status.

Missing data must not silently become zero. Model estimates must not be represented as verified physical events. Blockchain timestamps or signatures may support integrity of a record but do not prove that the underlying economic event occurred as claimed.

## 35. Consolidation and double-counting controls

At minimum:
- eliminate internal transactions in consolidated group accounts;
- distinguish intermediate inputs from final output;
- avoid counting asset transfers as new production;
- distinguish loan principal from income;
- distinguish collateral from cash;
- distinguish market capitalization from net asset value;
- record subsidies and public support transparently;
- account for displaced production and unpaid inputs where material;
- report gross and net measures side by side.

# Part X — Falsifiable hypotheses for Volume II

## 36. Initial research hypotheses

**H2-1 — Value-vector completeness.** A dashboard that keeps production, distribution, capability, resilience and ecological measures separate will identify trade-offs and failure modes that a single GDP/profit/market-capitalization metric misses.

**H2-2 — Production-function sensitivity.** Estimated productivity and input elasticities will vary materially across Cobb–Douglas, CES, Leontief and more flexible specifications in sectors with bottlenecks or limited substitutability.

**H2-3 — Capital-services distinction.** Models using productive capital services will explain production differences better than models using asset market values alone in settings where asset prices diverge from utilization and productive efficiency.

**H2-4 — Coordination complementarity.** Coordination quality will have a positive marginal effect where inputs are complementary and information delays are material, but the effect will be reduced or reversed when governance costs, congestion or capture rise.

**H2-5 — Network non-monotonicity.** Increasing connectivity will produce non-monotonic effects on net output and resilience as matching benefits compete with contagion, congestion and concentration.

**H2-6 — Counterfactual synergy.** Some apparent cooperative productivity gains will disappear after controlling for additional inputs, participant selection, subsidies, displacement and unpaid labour.

**H2-7 — Distribution-production feedback.** Distribution arrangements will affect future productivity through investment, health, skills, incentives, demand and trust; the direction and magnitude will vary by institutional context.

**H2-8 — Ecological constraint relevance.** Where critical natural assets approach thresholds, models that permit unrestricted substitution will produce materially different and potentially unsafe recommendations compared with constrained physical models.

**H2-9 — Multidimensional prosperity ranking.** Rankings of institutional arrangements will differ when capability, ownership, resilience and ecological indicators are included alongside income and output.

**H2-10 — MFP interpretation.** Treating measured multifactor productivity as a pure technology variable will misattribute some organizational, network, scale, market-power and measurement effects.

## 37. Experimental design

Each hypothesis must specify:
1. target population or sector;
2. treatment or institutional arrangement;
3. credible baseline;
4. primary and secondary outcomes;
5. resource and accounting boundaries;
6. time horizon;
7. identification strategy;
8. confounders and spillovers;
9. uncertainty and sensitivity;
10. predeclared refutation criteria.

A simulation can test internal consistency and explore mechanisms. It cannot establish empirical superiority unless calibrated and validated against observed data and competing models.

# Part XI — Research programme and implementation sequence

## 38. What this volume establishes

This volume establishes a conceptual and measurement architecture. It does **not** yet:
- select a single universal theory of value;
- provide a complete production function for every sector;
- settle the labour-value versus marginal-value debate;
- prove that cooperation always produces positive synergy;
- define a universal distributive formula;
- claim that a token or blockchain creates value;
- demonstrate empirically that a SYNERGY institution outperforms existing alternatives.

## 39. Required next work

1. Create a formal glossary and data dictionary for every value and production variable.
2. Develop a sector-specific production model for a small set of pilot domains.
3. Specify the counterfactual synergy estimator and its causal identification requirements.
4. Build a value-added and consolidated-balance-sheet reconciliation example.
5. Model value creation, capture and distribution as separate but connected processes.
6. Compare productive capital services with financial asset values.
7. Add physical resource and ecological accounts to the production model.
8. Connect the value vector to the contribution and distribution theory in Volume III.
9. Build a reproducible simulation benchmark before applying the theory to consortium cells.

## 40. Concluding proposition

The proposed contribution of SYNERGY is a disciplined multidimensional framework in which production, exchange, distribution, capabilities, networks and ecological conditions are measured as distinct but interacting parts of economic reality.

The central research hypothesis is that **sustainable prosperity depends on increasing real productive capability and beneficial coordination while preserving rights, accounting integrity, fair access, resilience and the natural systems on which production depends**.

This hypothesis must be tested against credible alternatives. If a simpler institutional arrangement produces better results under the same constraints, SYNERGY should adopt it. The theory's purpose is to discover effective economic mechanisms, not to defend a predetermined architecture.

## Statistical standards note

The 2025 SNA was adopted by the UN Statistical Commission in 2025. It updates the international conceptual framework, including treatment and guidance for digitalization, data, financial issues, natural resource depletion and well-being/sustainability-related analysis. Adoption of a standard does not mean every national statistical office has already implemented it. SYNERGY models must state which national-accounting vintage and country implementation they use. The SNA is a statistical accounting framework; it is not, by itself, a complete theory of social welfare or ecological sustainability.
