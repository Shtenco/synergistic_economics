## Abstract

This volume defines the conceptual and analytical foundations of SYNERGISTIC ECONOMICS. Its purpose is to establish what the proposed system studies, which constraints apply to every economic order, which institutional problems it seeks to address, and what principles should guide the design of economic mechanisms.

The central proposition is that durable prosperity depends not on one universal allocation mechanism, but on the interaction of several mechanisms: decentralized exchange, competition, cooperation, organizations, public institutions, common-resource governance, productive ownership and computational coordination. Each mechanism has capabilities and failure modes. Their appropriate scope depends on information, transaction costs, external effects, scale, uncertainty, power distribution and the rights at stake.

SYNERGY is therefore framed as a **polycentric, rights-constrained and evidence-driven economic architecture**. It is not a command economy, an unregulated market ideal, a token-based monetary promise or an assertion that AI can replace institutional judgment.

This volume develops:
- a working ontology of the economy;
- twelve foundational axioms;
- derived design principles;
- a framework for choosing the level and mechanism of coordination;
- a general dynamic model of the economy;
- a multi-objective concept of prosperity;
- research propositions and falsification criteria.

The axioms are explicit starting assumptions for system design, not claims that all economic theories already accept them in this exact form. The propositions derived from them must be tested against evidence and competing explanations.

# Part I. The object of the theory

## 1. What is an economy?

For this research program, an economy is:

> A changing system through which people and organizations mobilize limited resources, apply knowledge and capabilities, coordinate activities, create and exchange goods and services, allocate risks and benefits, maintain productive assets, and establish rules governing these processes across time.

This definition deliberately includes more than markets and money. Markets are one institutional mechanism within an economy. Money is one coordination technology. Firms, households, cooperatives, states, communities, networks and digital platforms are organizational forms. Property, contracts, norms and constitutions define rights and obligations. Natural systems provide indispensable inputs and absorb some residual effects of production.

An economy therefore has at least seven inseparable dimensions:

1. **Material:** land, energy, water, materials, equipment, infrastructure and physical outputs.
2. **Human:** labour, skills, health, knowledge, creativity, care and time.
3. **Informational:** prices, data, forecasts, standards, records and local knowledge.
4. **Institutional:** laws, contracts, norms, organizations, enforcement and dispute resolution.
5. **Financial:** money, credit, debt, equity, liquidity, savings and risk allocation.
6. **Relational:** trust, networks, cooperation, bargaining power and dependencies.
7. **Temporal and ecological:** investment, depreciation, uncertainty, regeneration, depletion and effects on future generations.

A theory that omits any of these dimensions may still be useful for a narrow question, but it cannot claim to describe the whole economic system.

## 2. The basic economic problem

The fundamental economic problem is not simply scarcity. It is the coordination of heterogeneous people, resources, knowledge and obligations under conditions of scarcity, uncertainty and interdependence.

Three questions arise in every economic system:

- **Allocation:** what should be produced, by whom, where, when and with which resources?
- **Coordination:** how do independent decisions fit together, and how are conflicts, dependencies and changing information handled?
- **Distribution:** who receives the outputs, income, ownership rights, opportunities and risks?

A fourth question is often neglected:

- **Reproduction:** how does the system maintain and renew the human, produced, institutional and natural assets on which future production depends?

A system can perform well on one question and poorly on another. It may produce a large output while distributing ownership narrowly; distribute current income while underinvesting in productive assets; coordinate efficiently under normal conditions while failing under shocks; or maximize short-term output while degrading the resource base.

SYNERGY treats allocation, coordination, distribution and reproduction as a connected design problem.

## 3. Economic systems as institutional configurations

An economic system should not be classified only by slogans such as “capitalist”, “socialist”, “market-based” or “planned”. For analytical purposes, it is more useful to describe a system by its institutional configuration.

For each important economic function, identify:

- who owns the relevant assets;
- who has decision rights;
- how information is generated and transmitted;
- how resources are allocated;
- how prices or non-price signals are formed;
- who bears risk and residual losses;
- how benefits are distributed;
- how rules are enforced;
- how participants can challenge decisions;
- how the system adapts when conditions change.

This functional description allows comparison across mixed economies. A country, sector or enterprise may use markets for some decisions, public provision for others, cooperative ownership for shared assets and hierarchical management for tightly coupled production.

**Research implication:** SYNERGY should be compared with alternative institutional configurations function by function, not only with ideological labels.

# Part II. Universal constraints and economic tensions

## 4. Twelve foundational constraints

The following constraints apply to any realistic economic architecture. They are not unique to SYNERGY.

### 4.1 Limited resources and competing uses

At a given time, resources are finite relative to all possible uses. Labour hours, capital equipment, land, energy, attention and ecological capacity cannot be allocated to every desirable activity simultaneously.

The opportunity cost of a decision is the value of the best alternative forgone. This means that even a system pursuing broad welfare must make trade-offs explicit.

### 4.2 Heterogeneous and dispersed knowledge

Relevant knowledge is distributed across individuals, firms, communities, researchers, public institutions and supply networks. Some knowledge is codified; some is local, tacit, time-sensitive or difficult to transmit.

No single actor should be assumed to possess all information required for every economic decision. This is a central insight in the analysis of decentralized coordination associated with Friedrich Hayek. It does not prove that markets solve every information problem; it establishes that knowledge aggregation is itself a major institutional challenge.

### 4.3 Heterogeneous preferences and capabilities

People differ in needs, preferences, skills, risk tolerance, time horizons, family responsibilities and opportunities. A uniform allocation rule may be administratively simple while producing poor results for diverse circumstances.

The system must distinguish equality of rights from identical treatment, and equal access from identical outcomes.

### 4.4 Interdependence and complementarities

Production increasingly depends on combinations of inputs and participants. A machine may be unproductive without maintenance, software, energy, trained operators and logistics. A research discovery may create little value without manufacturing, finance, standards and distribution.

The value of a contribution can therefore depend on the network in which it is used. This makes attribution and distribution difficult: the total output of a joint process cannot always be uniquely divided into independent individual contributions.

### 4.5 Transaction and coordination costs

Exchange is not free. Search, negotiation, contracting, measurement, verification, compliance, monitoring, enforcement and dispute resolution all consume resources.

Ronald Coase's analysis of transaction costs explains why real economies contain firms and other organizations rather than relying exclusively on spot markets. The relevant choice is not “market or organization” in the abstract, but which institutional arrangement coordinates a given activity at lower total cost while preserving other important values.

### 4.6 Uncertainty and incomplete information

Economic actors do not know all future prices, technologies, preferences, risks or policy changes. Some uncertainty can be estimated statistically; some cannot be reliably assigned a probability.

Institutions must therefore address not only expected efficiency but also robustness, insurance, reserves, experimentation, reversibility and recovery after failure.

### 4.7 Incentives and strategic behaviour

People and organizations respond to rules, rewards, penalties and opportunities. They may also hide information, shift costs, exploit loopholes, collude, manipulate metrics or seek control over rule-making.

A mechanism that works under perfect honesty may fail under strategic behaviour. Incentive compatibility, monitoring, transparency and credible enforcement are therefore core design questions.

### 4.8 Unequal bargaining power and concentration

Control over capital, infrastructure, data, platforms, essential services, legal expertise or information can shape the choices available to others.

Formal consent does not always imply meaningful bargaining equality. A system must examine both the existence of rights and the practical ability to exercise them.

### 4.9 Externalities and public goods

Some costs and benefits fall on people who are not parties to a transaction. Pollution, ecosystem depletion, knowledge spillovers, public health effects and network infrastructure are examples.

Private prices alone may not capture these effects. Institutions are needed to define responsibility, measure impacts and coordinate provision or protection.

### 4.10 Path dependence and institutional inertia

Past investments, legal structures, standards, networks and power relations constrain future choices. An institution may persist not because it is optimal, but because switching costs, vested interests or coordination barriers make change difficult.

Reform must therefore account for transition costs, legacy claims and credible implementation paths.

### 4.11 Ecological and intergenerational limits

Economic activity depends on ecological systems and material throughput. Some natural assets are substitutable only to a limited degree, and some ecological losses may be irreversible or involve threshold effects.

The World Bank's Changing Wealth of Nations work illustrates why GDP should be complemented by measures of the asset base supporting future production, including produced, human and natural capital. The report also discusses the limits of assuming that losses in one asset class can always be offset by gains in another.

### 4.12 Human dignity, rights and legitimacy

People are not merely production inputs or variables in an optimization function. They have rights, agency, relationships, privacy and claims to due process.

An economic system may not treat a person's basic dignity as a reward for measured productivity. Nor should an algorithmic or market outcome automatically override legal rights and legitimate procedures.

## 5. The central institutional tensions

The constraints above create recurring tensions. These are not necessarily contradictions that can be eliminated. They are trade-offs that institutions must manage.

| Tension | One side | Other side | SYNERGY research question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decentralization vs coordination | Local autonomy and dispersed knowledge | System-wide consistency and infrastructure | Which decisions should be local, shared or centrally standardized? |
| Incentives vs security | Rewards for effort, risk and innovation | Protection from catastrophic deprivation | How can dynamic incentives coexist with universal protections? |
| Private vs collective ownership | Autonomy, accountability and investment incentives | Shared benefits and broad access | Which assets and rights are best held individually, jointly or publicly? |
| Competition vs cooperation | Discovery, entry and pressure to improve | Trust, scale economies and shared capability | How can cooperation avoid collusion and competition avoid destructive fragmentation? |
| Efficiency vs resilience | Lean utilization and low cost | Redundancy, reserves and recovery capacity | What is the socially optimal balance under uncertainty? |
| Measurement vs autonomy | Comparable metrics and accountability | Privacy, qualitative judgment and freedom | What should be measured, by whom and with what consequences? |
| Automation vs human judgment | Speed, consistency and scale | Context, responsibility and appeal | Which decisions may be automated, and which require human authorization? |
| Present welfare vs future capacity | Current consumption and returns | Investment, maintenance and ecological regeneration | How should the system protect the asset base of future generations? |

A mature SYNERGY design should publish the trade-offs it makes. It should not conceal value judgments inside an optimization algorithm.

# Part III. What existing mechanisms can and cannot do

## 6. Markets

Markets can coordinate decentralized exchange through prices, contracts, entry and exit. They can aggregate some dispersed information and create incentives to discover lower-cost methods or products that people value.

However, market coordination depends on institutional conditions: enforceable rights, competition, reliable information, workable contracts and the ability to bear or insure risks. Markets may perform poorly where externalities are large, market power is durable, information is asymmetric, public goods are important or participants lack meaningful alternatives.

**SYNERGY position:** preserve market exchange where it provides useful discovery and coordination, while building institutions for competition, public goods, externalities, access and protection against market power.

## 7. Firms and hierarchical organizations

Firms coordinate activities through authority, routines, long-term relationships and internal planning. They can reduce repeated contracting and support investments requiring tightly coordinated teams.

But hierarchy can also create information bottlenecks, agency problems, internal politics, weak contestability and concentration of decision rights.

**SYNERGY position:** treat firms as useful coordination institutions, not as universally optimal or inherently illegitimate. Examine ownership, voice, accountability and the boundary between internal organization and external exchange.

## 8. Public institutions

Public institutions can establish legal rights, provide public goods, regulate externalities, maintain infrastructure, insure against systemic risks and protect universal guarantees.

They can also fail through capture, weak feedback, administrative overload, politicized allocation or poor implementation.

**SYNERGY position:** public authority is necessary for some functions, but must be bounded by law, transparency, contestability, institutional checks and evaluation.

## 9. Cooperatives and common-resource institutions

Cooperatives can align ownership and participation for members. Community institutions can govern shared resources when users have meaningful participation, clear rules, monitoring and conflict-resolution processes.

They can nevertheless face undercapitalization, internal conflict, free-riding, limited scale, elite capture or difficulty admitting new members.

Elinor Ostrom's work is important because it rejects the assumption that common resources must always be governed either by privatization or by a single central authority. Her research identifies conditions under which users can develop durable, locally adapted governance arrangements.

**SYNERGY position:** use cooperative and common ownership where shared benefits, user knowledge and collective stewardship justify it, while designing explicit rules for capital formation, entry, exit, monitoring and accountability.

## 10. Digital platforms and AI systems

Platforms reduce search and matching costs, coordinate multi-sided networks and make new services possible. AI can assist forecasting, classification, optimization and simulation.

But network effects, data concentration, opaque ranking, switching costs and control over interfaces can create dependency and private governance power. AI systems may reproduce data errors, optimize narrow metrics or make decisions that affected people cannot meaningfully challenge.

**SYNERGY position:** use digital systems as infrastructure for coordination, not as a substitute for legitimate institutions. Require interoperability where appropriate, data governance, auditability, bounded authority and appeal.

# Part IV. Foundational axioms of SYNERGY

## 11. Method of axiomatization

An axiom in this document is a foundational design proposition adopted for the purpose of constructing and testing the system. It is not necessarily a universal theorem or a statement accepted by every school of economics.

Each axiom has four parts:

1. **Statement:** the proposition.
2. **Reason:** why the framework adopts it.
3. **Design consequence:** what follows for institutions.
4. **Test or challenge:** what evidence could require revision.

## 12. The twelve axioms

### Axiom A1 — The axiom of finite resources

**Statement:** Economic systems operate under constraints of time, resources, productive capacity and ecological limits.

**Reason:** Desirable uses compete. Every allocation has an opportunity cost.

**Design consequence:** SYNERGY must use transparent resource accounting, opportunity-cost analysis, lifecycle costing and explicit constraints. It cannot promise unlimited real consumption from finite productive capacity.

**Test or challenge:** A model that repeatedly violates physical resource constraints or hides scarcity through accounting conventions is invalid.

### Axiom A2 — The axiom of dispersed knowledge

**Statement:** Relevant economic knowledge is distributed among many actors and cannot be assumed to be fully available to a single planner, firm, community or AI system.

**Reason:** Local and tacit knowledge changes continuously. Information collection and transmission have costs.

**Design consequence:** Preserve decentralized discovery, local decision rights, feedback and the ability to challenge central estimates. Use AI to assist aggregation, not to claim omniscience.

**Test or challenge:** Compare local, market, hierarchical and AI-assisted mechanisms under different information conditions.

### Axiom A3 — The axiom of human heterogeneity

**Statement:** Participants have different needs, capabilities, preferences, risks and circumstances.

**Reason:** Identical rules can produce unequal practical opportunities when starting conditions differ.

**Design consequence:** Protect universal rights while allowing plural contracts, services, participation routes and context-sensitive allocation.

**Test or challenge:** Evaluate distributional effects across different groups and circumstances rather than only average outcomes.

### Axiom A4 — The axiom of interdependence

**Statement:** A significant share of economic output depends on interactions among complementary resources, people, institutions and networks.

**Reason:** Isolated input attribution often fails to explain joint production.

**Design consequence:** Model economic relationships and complementarities; measure synergy against explicit counterfactuals; avoid arbitrary claims that one actor created all joint value.

**Test or challenge:** Test whether measured joint gains persist after controlling for input quality, selection, time and risk.

### Axiom A5 — The axiom of institutional mediation

**Statement:** Economic outcomes depend not only on resources and technology but also on the institutions that define, allocate and enforce rights.

**Reason:** Contracts, property rules, norms, enforcement and organizational boundaries shape incentives and transaction costs.

**Design consequence:** Treat institutional design as a productive factor in the broad analytical sense. Document rules, enforcement, appeal and accountability.

**Test or challenge:** Compare otherwise similar activities under different institutional arrangements.

### Axiom A6 — The axiom of incentive responsiveness

**Statement:** Participants respond to incentives and may behave strategically when rules create exploitable opportunities.

**Reason:** A system must work under realistic behaviour, not only under perfect altruism or perfect information.

**Design consequence:** Design for incentive compatibility, fraud resistance, clear liability, monitoring and correction. Do not rely solely on moral appeals.

**Test or challenge:** Red-team mechanisms against gaming, collusion, adverse selection and moral hazard.

### Axiom A7 — The axiom of uncertainty

**Statement:** Future conditions are not fully knowable, and some risks cannot be represented reliably by a single probability distribution.

**Reason:** Technology, preferences, shocks and institutional responses change.

**Design consequence:** Preserve reserves, diversification, reversibility, experimentation and contingency plans. Avoid optimizing only for one forecast.

**Test or challenge:** Stress-test policies under scenarios outside the calibration range.

### Axiom A8 — The axiom of power asymmetry

**Statement:** Control over scarce assets, infrastructure, information and rule-making can create bargaining power beyond the value of an individual transaction.

**Reason:** Formal market participation does not guarantee meaningful choice or contestability.

**Design consequence:** Monitor concentration of ownership, control, access and information. Provide anti-capture safeguards and alternative routes where dependency becomes excessive.

**Test or challenge:** Measure whether a participant can realistically switch providers, contest decisions or enter a market.

### Axiom A9 — The axiom of external effects

**Statement:** Economic decisions can impose material costs or benefits on people and systems outside the immediate transaction.

**Reason:** Private incentives do not always internalize social, ecological or systemic effects.

**Design consequence:** Identify affected parties, measure external effects where feasible and define responsibility, compensation, limits or collective provision.

**Test or challenge:** Compare private returns with lifecycle and social outcomes, including uncertainty.

### Axiom A10 — The axiom of dynamic learning

**Statement:** Economic institutions must adapt because technologies, knowledge, preferences and constraints change.

**Reason:** A static allocation rule can become inefficient or unjust when conditions change.

**Design consequence:** Build feedback, review, sunset clauses, institutional experimentation and versioned policy changes into the architecture.

**Test or challenge:** Evaluate whether the system corrects errors and learns faster than it accumulates institutional rigidity.

### Axiom A11 — The axiom of human dignity and rights

**Statement:** Basic rights, due process and human dignity are constraints on economic optimization, not rewards allocated by productivity scores.

**Reason:** People are rights-bearing agents, not merely instruments of production.

**Design consequence:** Define non-negotiable rights, privacy boundaries, consent, appeal and limits on automated decisions.

**Test or challenge:** Reject any optimization that improves aggregate output by violating protected rights or denying due process.

### Axiom A12 — The axiom of intergenerational continuity

**Statement:** An economy must maintain the productive and ecological foundations required for future generations to retain meaningful opportunities.

**Reason:** Current output can rise while underlying assets are depleted.

**Design consequence:** Track changes in produced, human, institutional and natural assets; distinguish renewable from non-renewable resources; disclose assumptions about substitution.

**Test or challenge:** Examine whether real comprehensive wealth and critical natural assets are maintained, not only whether current GDP or income rises.

# Part V. Principles derived from the axioms

## 13. From axioms to design principles

The axioms do not prescribe one institutional form. They constrain the design space.

### Principle D1 — Polycentric coordination

Derived from A2, A4, A5 and A10.

Use multiple decision centres with clearly defined jurisdictions. Local actors should decide matters for which they possess relevant knowledge and bear consequences. Shared or higher-level institutions should coordinate spillovers, standards, systemic risks and common infrastructure.

### Principle D2 — Mechanism pluralism

Derived from A1, A2, A4, A5 and A7.

No allocation mechanism should be presumed universally superior. Markets, contracts, hierarchies, public provision, cooperative governance, auctions, matching systems and AI-assisted optimization should be compared according to the task and conditions.

### Principle D3 — Rights-constrained optimization

Derived from A3 and A11.

Economic optimization operates inside a rights framework. Rights are not simply another weighted objective that can be traded away whenever a model predicts a larger aggregate benefit.

### Principle D4 — Broad access to productive opportunity

Derived from A3, A4, A8 and A12.

A prosperous system should expand meaningful access to skills, tools, infrastructure, capital, markets and participation. This does not require identical ownership shares or identical outcomes.

### Principle D5 — Accountable ownership

Derived from A5, A6, A8 and A11.

Every important asset and financial claim should have identifiable rights, obligations, control boundaries, encumbrances, responsible parties and dispute procedures.

### Principle D6 — Internalization and stewardship

Derived from A1, A9 and A12.

The cost of production should include relevant maintenance, depletion and external effects. Shared and natural assets require explicit stewardship obligations.

### Principle D7 — Adaptive institutional design

Derived from A7 and A10.

Rules should be reviewable and amendable through legitimate procedures. Experiments should be reversible where possible, and failed policies should be capable of being stopped.

### Principle D8 — Contestable power

Derived from A2, A8 and A11.

No actor should possess unchecked control over essential economic infrastructure, data, rule-making or dispute resolution. Power must be bounded, observable and challengeable.

### Principle D9 — Multi-dimensional performance

Derived from A1, A3, A9 and A12.

Evaluate production, distribution, opportunity, resilience, rights and ecological continuity together. Do not collapse all outcomes into a single score without disclosing assumptions and trade-offs.

### Principle D10 — Evidence before scale

Derived from A6, A7 and A10.

Institutional changes should progress through explicit hypotheses, limited pilots, independent evaluation and replication. Adoption should depend on evidence, not narrative confidence.

# Part VI. A decision framework for choosing mechanisms

## 14. The coordination problem

For each economic activity, SYNERGY asks which institutional mechanism should coordinate it. The answer depends on at least eight variables:

- **K — Knowledge distribution:** how dispersed and local is the relevant information?
- **T — Transaction costs:** how expensive is search, negotiation, contracting and enforcement?
- **E — External effects:** how much do costs or benefits fall on third parties?
- **C — Complementarity:** how tightly must inputs and decisions be coordinated?
- **M — Market power:** can one or a few actors control access or terms?
- **R — Risk and uncertainty:** how severe are downside risks and how hard are they to estimate?
- **S — Scale and scope:** are there strong economies of scale, network effects or shared infrastructure?
- **H — Rights sensitivity:** how directly does the decision affect basic rights, privacy or essential access?

The mechanism should be selected after evaluating these variables, not by ideological preference.

## 15. A preliminary mechanism-selection matrix

| Mechanism | Often useful when | Main risks to examine |
|---|---|---|
| Open market exchange | Goods are separable, entry is possible, prices convey useful information, externalities are manageable | Market power, exclusion, information asymmetry, externalities |
| Competitive procurement or auction | Specifications are measurable and multiple qualified suppliers exist | Collusion, gaming, incomplete specifications, winner's curse |
| Long-term contract | Investment and delivery require predictable obligations | Lock-in, renegotiation power, incomplete contracts |
| Firm hierarchy | Inputs are tightly coupled and rapid coordinated action matters | Bureaucracy, agency costs, weak voice, internal capture |
| Cooperative governance | Users or workers share durable interests and can monitor one another | Underinvestment, free-riding, elite capture, member conflict |
| Common-pool governance | Resource users can define boundaries, rules and monitoring | Overuse, exclusion, enforcement failures |
| Public provision | Universal access, public goods or rights-based service obligations dominate | Political capture, weak feedback, administrative inefficiency |
| Regulated private provision | Private execution can work under enforceable public obligations | Regulatory capture, weak enforcement, hidden costs |
| Platform coordination | Search, matching and network effects materially reduce costs | Dependency, data concentration, opaque ranking, gatekeeping |
| AI-assisted coordination | Large datasets and complex constraints can improve prediction or matching | Model error, bias, manipulation, opacity, automation overreach |
| Hybrid arrangement | Multiple mechanisms solve different parts of the problem | Interface failures, unclear accountability, coordination overhead |

This matrix is a research heuristic, not a deterministic classifier. Real activities often require hybrid arrangements.

## 16. The subsidiarity test

Before moving a decision to a higher level of coordination, ask:

1. Can the affected participants make the decision themselves?
2. Do they have enough information and capacity?
3. Are there significant spillovers onto outsiders?
4. Is a common standard or shared infrastructure necessary?
5. Would higher-level coordination reduce total costs or systemic risk?
6. Can the higher-level authority be audited and challenged?
7. Can the decision be returned to a lower level if centralization proves unnecessary?

The goal is neither maximum decentralization nor maximum centralization. It is **appropriate allocation of decision rights**.

# Part VII. The general mathematical model

## 17. Economy as a dynamic, multi-layer network

Let the economy at time \(t\) be represented by:

\[
\mathcal{E}_t = (P_t, A_t, R_t, I_t, G_t, X_t)
\]

where:

- \(P_t\) is the set of participants and organizations;
- \(A_t\) is the set of assets, resources and liabilities;
- \(R_t\) is the set of relationships, contracts, transactions and dependencies;
- \(I_t\) is the institutional rule system;
- \(G_t\) is the governance and decision-rights structure;
- \(X_t\) is the external state: technology, ecology, demographics, shocks and other conditions.

The system evolves according to:

\[
\mathcal{E}_{t+1} =
\Phi(\mathcal{E}_t, a_t, \varepsilon_t)
\]

where \(a_t\) represents decisions by participants and institutions, while \(\varepsilon_t\) represents shocks and unanticipated changes.

This notation does not assume that the economy can be perfectly observed. In practice, the model operates with incomplete data, measurement error and uncertain parameters.

## 18. Participants and heterogeneous objectives

Let each participant \(i\) have a state:

\[
s_i = (r_i, k_i, h_i, \theta_i, \rho_i, \ell_i)
\]

where:

- \(r_i\) = resource endowment;
- \(k_i\) = skills and knowledge;
- \(h_i\) = health, time and relevant capabilities;
- \(\theta_i\) = preferences and goals;
- \(\rho_i\) = risk exposure and tolerance;
- \(\ell_i\) = legal and institutional rights and obligations.

A participant's objective should not be presumed identical to that of every other participant. A firm, household, cooperative, public agency and individual may optimize different objectives under different constraints.

## 19. Production and complementarities

For project \(j\), a general production relation can be written:

\[
y_j = F_j(K_j, L_j, H_j, D_j, N_j, M_j; \tau_j)
\]

where \(K\) is produced capital, \(L\) labour, \(H\) knowledge, \(D\) data and digital resources, \(N\) network inputs, \(M\) materials and energy, and \(\tau\) technology and organizational conditions.

The function \(F_j\) is project-specific. There is no presumption that one universal functional form accurately describes all production.

Complementarities can be represented locally by interaction terms:

\[
y_j = F_j(x_j) + \sum_{m<n}\beta_{mn}x_mx_n + \epsilon_j
\]

The interaction coefficients are empirical parameters. Positive interaction terms must not be assumed in advance: combinations can also create congestion, coordination burdens or diminishing returns.

## 20. Transaction and coordination costs

For a mechanism \(m\), define total coordination cost as:

\[
TC_m = C^{search}_m + C^{negotiation}_m + C^{measurement}_m
+ C^{monitoring}_m + C^{enforcement}_m + C^{adaptation}_m
\]

This is an analytical decomposition, not a claim that each component is always independently observable.

A mechanism's performance should be evaluated using total social cost, including relevant external effects and institutional maintenance, not merely the private administrative cost of one party.

## 21. The constrained multi-objective problem

SYNERGY does not begin by assuming that all outcomes can be reduced to a single welfare number.

A general policy problem is:

\[
\max_{\pi} \; \mathbf{W}(\pi)
=
\left(
W_{prod},
W_{security},
W_{opportunity},
W_{distribution},
W_{resilience},
W_{ecology},
W_{agency}
\right)
\]

subject to:

\[
g_k(\pi,\mathcal{E}_t) \leq 0
\]

and rights constraints:

\[
h_q(\pi,\mathcal{E}_t) = \text{protected}
\]

Here \(\pi\) is a policy or institutional configuration. The vector \(\mathbf{W}\) contains distinct objectives. The constraints include resource limits, solvency, liquidity, ecological thresholds, legal duties and protected rights.

A scalar objective may be used for a particular experiment only if its weights and trade-offs are explicitly justified. Rights should not be treated as freely exchangeable weights.

## 22. Pareto efficiency is not sufficient

A state is Pareto efficient if no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off, under the specified preference and feasibility assumptions.

This is a useful concept, but it does not by itself answer questions about:
- initial endowments;
- bargaining power;
- rights;
- distribution of opportunity;
- externalities omitted from the model;
- future generations;
- unequal capabilities.

SYNERGY therefore treats efficiency as one important criterion, not a complete theory of justice or prosperity.

## 23. A wealth-maintenance condition

Let \(W_t^{real}\) represent a consistently defined measure of real comprehensive wealth for a specified population and accounting boundary.

A basic sustainability diagnostic is:

\[
\Delta \left(\frac{W_t^{real}}{N_t}\right) \geq 0
\]

where \(N_t\) is population.

This condition is not sufficient for sustainability. Aggregate wealth measures may conceal distributional losses, critical ecological thresholds, non-substitutable assets or measurement uncertainty. It is a diagnostic that must be supplemented with disaggregated asset and rights indicators.

# Part VIII. The constitutional logic

## 24. Why the constitution comes before the optimizer

An economic optimizer can calculate a policy that maximizes a specified objective under specified constraints. It cannot independently determine which rights should exist, who has legitimate authority, what counts as acceptable risk or which trade-offs society should permit.

Those are constitutional and political questions.

The SYNERGY constitutional layer should therefore define:

1. fundamental rights and prohibited actions;
2. ownership categories and enforceable claims;
3. jurisdiction and decision authority;
4. financial and fiscal limits;
5. transparency, privacy and data rights;
6. audit, appeal and dispute resolution;
7. emergency powers and their expiry;
8. amendment procedures;
9. liability for errors and misconduct;
10. independent oversight.

Only after these boundaries are defined should AI or other optimization systems operate within delegated authority.

## 25. The separation of powers

The system should avoid giving one institution simultaneous unchecked control over:

- rule creation;
- asset custody;
- valuation;
- allocation;
- enforcement;
- audit;
- dispute resolution.

Where practical, these functions should be separated or subject to independent review. Separation is not an end in itself: the goal is to reduce conflicts of interest, hidden discretion and unreviewable power.

## 26. AI as a bounded institutional actor

AI may:
- forecast;
- recommend;
- match;
- simulate;
- detect anomalies;
- execute pre-authorized low-risk operations;
- provide explanations and scenario comparisons.

AI must not, by default:
- redefine constitutional rights;
- create unlimited liabilities;
- confiscate assets;
- determine a person's basic entitlement through an opaque score;
- adjudicate its own disputes;
- control the audit process that evaluates it;
- change its own authority boundaries.

High-impact actions require a defined authorization path, human responsibility, audit logs, a fallback mechanism and an appeal route.

| F1 | Institutional performance depends on the fit between the coordination mechanism and the structure of the task. | Compare mechanisms across tasks with different information, transaction-cost and externality profiles. |
| F2 | Hybrid arrangements can outperform single-mechanism arrangements when different task components have different coordination requirements. | Factorial simulations and case comparisons with explicit interface costs. |
| F3 | Better access to local information improves decisions when local information is material and can be transmitted reliably. | Randomized information access or matched decision experiments. |
| F4 | Accountability and appeal mechanisms can improve legitimacy and error correction in high-impact allocation systems. | Compare correction rates, participant trust and decision quality. |
| F5 | Competition is more likely to discipline providers when entry, switching and interoperability are credible. | Measure entry, switching costs, concentration and quality response. |
| F6 | Cooperation is more durable when membership, contribution, monitoring and dispute rules are clear. | Compare governance arrangements across cooperative and commons cases. |
| F7 | Long-term prosperity is better assessed using both output flows and asset stocks. | Compare GDP/output trends with comprehensive wealth and asset condition. |
| F8 | Rights constraints reduce the set of feasible optimizations but can improve legitimacy and protect against unacceptable harms. | Compare constrained and unconstrained decision systems using pre-specified rights criteria. |
| F9 | Resilience requires explicit valuation of redundancy, reserves and recovery capacity. | Stress tests comparing lean and buffered configurations. |
| F10 | AI improves coordination only when data quality, model validity, incentives and institutional accountability are adequate. | Compare AI and non-AI baselines across data and governance conditions. |

These propositions are not established results. Each needs a research protocol, operational definitions and an explicit account of confounders.

## 28. Falsification and revision

The foundational framework must be revisable. It should be weakened or modified if evidence shows that:

- the proposed institutional pluralism creates coordination overhead greater than its benefits;
- the selected rights framework cannot be made operational or legitimate;
- polycentric arrangements consistently fail to control spillovers or systemic risks;
- AI-assisted mechanisms do not outperform simpler alternatives in the settings where they are proposed;
- measurement systems create persistent gaming or harmful exclusion;
- the proposed governance checks make decision-making too slow for the relevant environment;
- comprehensive wealth indicators conceal critical ecological or distributional losses;
- the architecture cannot be implemented without unsustainable subsidies, hidden liabilities or coercive control.

A theory that cannot specify how it could be wrong is not yet a testable research program.

# Part X. Research methodology and next steps

## 29. Standards for evidence

Every major claim in the repository should be labeled as one of the following:

- **Definition:** a term fixed for clarity within the project.
- **Normative principle:** a value commitment or institutional goal.
- **Theoretical proposition:** a logically argued relationship.
- **Empirical finding:** a result reported by an identifiable study or dataset.
- **Hypothesis:** a proposition awaiting testing.
- **Design proposal:** an implementation option not yet validated.
- **Simulation result:** an outcome produced under stated model assumptions, not direct proof about the real world.

The repository should maintain source links, publication dates, methods, limitations and the exact claims each source supports.

## 30. Research sequence

### Work package 1 — Conceptual consistency
- Build a canonical glossary.
- Resolve ambiguous uses of value, wealth, capital, ownership, contribution and prosperity.
- Map dependencies among the ten volumes.
- Identify propositions that conflict or require boundary conditions.

### Work package 2 — Literature review
- Review market coordination and dispersed knowledge.
- Review transaction-cost and institutional economics.
- Review common-pool resources and polycentric governance.
- Review welfare, capabilities and beyond-GDP measurement.
- Review organizational economics, cooperative ownership and platform governance.
- Record competing explanations, not only supportive evidence.

### Work package 3 — Formal specification
- Define agents, assets, rights, contracts and institutions.
- Specify production, exchange, distribution and accumulation.
- Represent externalities, uncertainty and power concentration.
- Specify the objective vector and hard constraints.

### Work package 4 — Comparative simulation
- Build baseline institutional models.
- Introduce heterogeneous agents and incomplete information.
- Model transaction costs, entry, exit, defaults and shocks.
- Compare single-mechanism and hybrid arrangements.
- Publish code, assumptions and sensitivity analysis.

### Work package 5 — Institutional validation
- Study real cases of firms, markets, cooperatives, public infrastructure and digital platforms.
- Separate selection effects from institutional effects.
- Identify conditions under which each mechanism succeeds or fails.

### Work package 6 — Protocol design
- Translate only sufficiently specified mechanisms into software.
- Keep legal rights and financial claims distinct from technical records.
- Build audit, appeal, privacy and failure-recovery paths before scaling.

# Part XI. Open questions

## 32. Questions that must remain open until researched

1. Which economic functions are best coordinated through prices, and which require non-market mechanisms?
2. How should the system identify and measure coordination costs without rewarding bureaucratic expansion?
3. How can collective ownership attract long-term capital while protecting member control?
4. What is the right balance between individual autonomy and collective obligations?
5. How can contribution measurement account for joint production without creating a false precision?
6. Which assets are critical and non-substitutable for ecological and social continuity?
7. How should the system handle disagreement about welfare weights and public priorities?
8. What forms of decentralization genuinely distribute power rather than merely distribute technical infrastructure?
9. How can AI-assisted decisions remain contestable when models are complex and data are sensitive?
10. What evidence would justify scaling a successful local institution to a regional or global network?

These questions are not defects in the framework. They define the research agenda.

## Conclusion

The foundation of SYNERGISTIC ECONOMICS is not the assertion that cooperation should replace competition, that AI should replace institutions, or that collective ownership should replace individual initiative.

It is the proposition that economic systems should be designed around the actual structure of economic problems: finite resources, dispersed knowledge, heterogeneous people, interdependent production, transaction costs, uncertainty, strategic behaviour, unequal power, external effects and intergenerational constraints.

From these conditions follows a disciplined design approach:

- use markets where decentralized exchange and competition work well;
- use organizations where coordinated authority reduces total costs;
- use cooperation where shared production and stewardship create mutual gains;
- use public institutions where rights, public goods and systemic risks require collective rules;
- use AI where it demonstrably improves analysis or coordination;
- constrain every mechanism through rights, transparency, accountability and review.

**The objective is not maximum centralization, maximum decentralization, maximum output or maximum redistribution in isolation. It is a durable institutional architecture that expands productive capacity, human agency, broad opportunity and resilience while respecting rights and maintaining the asset base on which future prosperity depends.**
