## Abstract

This volume develops a theory of contribution, ownership and distribution for a polycentric economy in which productive outcomes are often jointly created by people, firms, capital providers, infrastructure operators, knowledge contributors, public institutions, communities and ecosystems.

Its central proposition is deliberately restrictive: **contribution, ownership, entitlement, income and human worth are different things**. A measurement of contribution cannot by itself establish legal ownership, determine a person's basic rights, prove causation, or settle every distributive dispute. Ownership may arise from lawful acquisition, contract, inheritance, collective constitution, public trust or other legitimate institutions. Income may arise from wages, rents, interest, dividends, fees, transfers or residual claims. These categories must be recorded separately before they can be reconciled.

The volume proposes a four-layer architecture:

1. **Value creation:** identify the output, outcomes, costs, externalities and counterfactual surplus.
2. **Contribution attribution:** estimate the role of different inputs and actors without pretending that all contributions are independently observable.
3. **Rights and obligations:** establish enforceable ownership, contracts, liabilities, governance rights, duties and risk-bearing arrangements.
4. **Value distribution:** apply the agreed rules to the legally and economically distributable pool, subject to rights, solvency, reserves and social/ecological constraints.

The proposed SYNERGY Contribution Index (SCI) is a project-level analytical instrument, not a score of human worth. Universal Economic Participation (UEP) is treated as an institutional access architecture, not as an unconditional promise of investment returns. The Universal Opportunity Fund (UOF) is designed to expand productive capabilities and access, with transparent eligibility, additionality, risk controls and evaluation.

No single formula is declared universally just. The volume compares contractual, ownership-based, equal, needs-based, contribution-based, bargaining-based and cooperative-game allocation methods. It identifies where each may be appropriate, where it fails, and how a consortium can combine them transparently without disguising normative choices as neutral mathematics.

## 1. Scope and central distinctions

### 1.1 The central problem

Modern production is deeply interdependent. A product may depend on a designer, a production worker, a financier, a logistics network, a software platform, public roads, scientific knowledge, legal institutions, unpaid care and natural resources. A simple one-person/one-output model is rarely sufficient.

Yet the fact that many actors contribute to an outcome does not imply that every actor owns an equal share of every asset or has the same contractual claim. Nor does it mean that an algorithm can infer a uniquely correct distribution from transaction records.

The research problem is therefore not “find the one true contribution score”. It is:

> How can institutions identify relevant contributions, respect legitimate rights, share jointly generated surplus under explicit rules, and prevent the measurement and distribution system from becoming a source of domination?

### 1.2 Definitions that must not be collapsed

| Concept | Meaning | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Contribution | An input or action plausibly helping produce an outcome | Ownership, moral worth or an automatic income claim |
| Causal effect | Difference in an outcome attributable to an intervention under a defined counterfactual | Legal entitlement or complete moral desert |
| Contractual claim | A payment or performance right created by an enforceable agreement | General ownership of the enterprise |
| Ownership | A legally or institutionally recognized bundle of rights over an asset or entity | That the owner personally created the asset |
| Governance right | Authority to participate in specified decisions | A proportional share of all income |
| Risk-bearing | Exposure to loss under a defined arrangement | Unlimited liability or automatic control |
| Income | A flow received by a person or entity over a period | Net wealth creation or productive contribution |
| Wealth | A stock of assets less liabilities, under a defined valuation basis | Liquidity or current income |
| Surplus | Residual after specified costs, obligations and accounting boundaries | A free pool before claims and reserves |
| Human worth | Equal moral standing of persons | A measurable economic performance variable |

### 1.3 Normative boundary

No SCI score, productivity metric, reputation score, AI prediction, token balance or market valuation may determine:
- basic rights, legal personhood or access to due process;
- the minimum protections guaranteed by applicable law;
- a person's dignity, social status or moral worth;
- access to emergency protection or essential services where these are rights-based;
- whether a person may challenge a decision or leave a voluntary association.

The system may use verified performance data to allocate a narrowly defined project reward where the rules were disclosed in advance and are contestable. It may not convert that reward instrument into a universal ranking of people.

## 2. Four-layer distribution architecture

### 2.1 Layer A — Value creation

The project first defines its accounting and causal boundary:
- unit of analysis: person, project, firm, consortium-cell, network or ecosystem;
- period and time horizon;
- outputs and outcomes;
- monetary and non-monetary measures;
- direct costs, capital consumption and financing costs;
- externalities and transferred costs;
- uncertainty and counterfactual baseline.

The distributable pool must not be confused with gross sales, token issuance, TVL, market capitalization or a project's estimated social value.

A simplified financial bridge is:

\[
\text{Operating surplus}=\text{recognized revenue}-\text{recognized operating costs}
\]

\[
\text{Distributable cash}=\text{cash available}-\text{taxes}-\text{due liabilities}
-\text{required reserves}-\text{approved reinvestment}
\]

The actual accounting treatment must follow the relevant legal entity, accounting standards, insolvency rules and contract. A positive accounting profit does not guarantee cash availability; cash receipts do not automatically constitute earned income; unrealized asset appreciation is not automatically distributable cash.

### 2.2 Layer B — Contribution attribution

This layer asks which inputs plausibly changed the outcome and with what uncertainty. It records evidence, not final entitlement.

Possible input classes include:
- labour and operational execution;
- design, invention and problem formulation;
- management, coordination and conflict resolution;
- capital supplied and capital services;
- risk-bearing and guarantees;
- knowledge, data and reusable intellectual assets;
- maintenance, reliability and security;
- care, training and capability development;
- network access and market development;
- public infrastructure and publicly funded research;
- community participation and local knowledge;
- ecological resources and ecosystem services.

These categories are not automatically additive. If a team jointly develops one indivisible result, separately scoring each participant's apparent output can double-count the same joint product.

### 2.3 Layer C — Rights and obligations

This layer specifies:
- legal title and beneficial ownership;
- licences and intellectual-property rights;
- wage, fee, royalty and dividend claims;
- debt, collateral, guarantees and priority;
- governance and voting rights;
- fiduciary duties and conflict rules;
- exit, transfer, buyout and dissolution rights;
- liability, insurance and loss allocation;
- privacy, data access and consent;
- public, community and ecological restrictions.

A digital ledger can document a claim, transfer or vote. It cannot by itself establish that the underlying contract is valid, that the signer had authority, that a physical asset exists, or that a real-world title is legally enforceable.

### 2.4 Layer D — Value distribution

Distribution rules apply only after the pool and enforceable claims have been established. A transparent allocation sequence might be:

1. satisfy legally senior obligations and mandatory protections;
2. pay due wages, supplier claims and contractually defined fees;
3. service debt according to its terms;
4. compensate agreed capital services and risk-bearing where due;
5. fund reserves, maintenance and approved reinvestment;
6. allocate residual surplus under the project's pre-agreed distribution constitution;
7. allocate any collective dividend according to the collective asset's own rules.

This is a design sequence, not a universal priority rule: actual law, insolvency status, security interests, labour law and contract may alter the order. A project must publish its governing waterfall before participants rely on it.

## 3. A multidimensional theory of contribution

### 3.1 Contribution is context-dependent

An input is economically relevant only relative to a specified outcome, time horizon, counterfactual and production system. The same activity can be critical in one project and irrelevant in another. A maintenance intervention may produce no visible output today while preventing a costly failure next month. A public road may support thousands of firms, while its contribution cannot sensibly be assigned as a simple percentage of each firm's revenue.

Therefore, contribution is a relation:

\[
C_{i,k}=g(i,\,k,\,t,\,\mathcal{B},\,\mathcal{M},\,\mathcal{E})
\]

where:
- \(i\) is the actor or input;
- \(k\) is the outcome dimension;
- \(t\) is the time horizon;
- \(\mathcal{B}\) is the system boundary;
- \(\mathcal{M}\) is the counterfactual or causal model;
- \(\mathcal{E}\) is the evidence set.

There is no context-free contribution quantity.

### 3.2 Contribution classes and evidence

| Contribution class | Possible evidence | Main attribution risk |
|---|---|---|
| Direct labour | Verified tasks, hours, accepted work, output quality | Hours mistaken for value; invisible work |
| Specialized expertise | Validated deliverables, peer review, avoided errors | Credential bias; overclaiming |
| Coordination | Reduced delays, resolved dependencies, team outcomes | Credit capture by visible managers |
| Capital | Amount, duration, seniority, risk and actual loss exposure | Principal confused with contribution or profit |
| Innovation | Tested improvement, adoption, incremental outcome | Patent count or novelty mistaken for impact |
| Maintenance | Uptime, incident prevention, asset life | Prevention is counterfactual and hard to observe |
| Care and training | Time, outcomes, capability development | Unpaid and relational work omitted |
| Data and knowledge | Provenance, lawful use, quality, reuse and consent | Privacy violation; data quantity mistaken for value |
| Network contribution | New counterparties, trust, interoperability, access | Double-counting network effects |
| Public/common inputs | Infrastructure, research, education, legal framework | Arbitrary assignment of public value to private actors |
| Ecological inputs | Resource use, regeneration, ecosystem condition | Market price omits depletion and damage |

Evidence should be graded by provenance, completeness, independence, reproducibility, time relevance and susceptibility to gaming. A record of activity is not proof of impact.

### 3.3 Complementarity and joint production

If two inputs are complements, their joint output can exceed the sum of their isolated outputs. For a production function \(Y=f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\), complementarity between inputs \(i\) and \(j\) may be represented locally by:

\[
\frac{\partial^2Y}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}>0
\]

But the sign and magnitude may vary by scale, technology and operating conditions. The interaction term cannot simply be assigned to both actors in full. Doing so double-counts synergy.

A practical ledger should distinguish:
- direct attributable output;
- interaction or joint-production surplus;
- shared infrastructure contribution;
- external benefits and costs;
- uncertainty and unresolved attribution.

A negotiated rule can distribute the joint surplus, but the choice of rule is a constitutional or contractual decision, not a mathematical discovery.

### 3.4 Time, maintenance and delayed effects

Contribution windows should match the production cycle. Short measurement windows systematically under-reward:
- preventive maintenance;
- training and mentoring;
- research with delayed results;
- resilience and redundancy;
- relationship-building;
- ecological restoration;
- long-horizon capital formation.

A project may use rolling attribution windows and deferred reward accounts, but deferred claims must be disclosed, bounded, auditable and treated correctly under insolvency and employment law.

### 3.5 Negative contribution and external harm

A contribution system must not treat gross output as positive regardless of consequences. It should separately record:
- rework and quality failures;
- safety incidents and preventable harm;
- fraud, abuse and deliberate sabotage;
- environmental damage;
- privacy and security violations;
- costs shifted to other cells or future periods.

Sanctions and liability must follow due process and applicable law. An algorithmic negative score is not a substitute for investigation, proportionality or appeal.

## 4. Ownership is a bundle of rights, not a contribution score

### 4.1 Plural ownership

SYNERGY recognizes multiple ownership forms that may coexist:
1. **Personal ownership:** assets held by individuals or households.
2. **Enterprise ownership:** equity or membership interests in firms and cooperatives.
3. **Project ownership:** rights over a defined project entity or its assets.
4. **Collective ownership:** assets held for a defined group under a constitution.
5. **Network/common ownership:** shared infrastructure, standards, knowledge pools or protocols.
6. **Public ownership or trusteeship:** assets held by public bodies or in trust for a public purpose.
7. **Ecological/common-resource regimes:** rights and duties constrained by regeneration, access and stewardship rules.

No form should be presumed optimal in every context. The relevant question is which bundle of rights best supports investment, access, accountability, adaptability and protection against capture.

### 4.2 Decompose the ownership bundle

For every asset, specify who holds:
- possession or operational control;
- use rights;
- exclusion rights;
- income rights;
- transfer rights;
- residual control rights;
- governance rights;
- maintenance obligations;
- liability and loss exposure;
- rights on dissolution.

A token that represents one of these rights must identify the underlying legal instrument, issuer, governing law, transfer restrictions, redemption or enforcement process, and what happens on insolvency. Tokenization alone does not create ownership.

### 4.3 Contribution does not rewrite title

A person can contribute to an asset without automatically acquiring legal title to it. Conversely, a lawful owner may receive income without personally producing the asset's current output. Whether that arrangement is fair, efficient or socially desirable is a separate institutional question.

The system should not retroactively alter ownership based on a score generated after the work was performed. Any participation-based ownership scheme must state:
- the eligible activity and measurement period;
- vesting and forfeiture conditions;
- transfer and dilution rules;
- tax and legal treatment;
- governance rights;
- dispute and appeal process;
- treatment of illness, disability, care leave and involuntary interruption.

### 4.4 Capital and risk

Capital compensation should distinguish:
- return of principal;
- time value or capital service;
- risk premium;
- realized loss;
- control rights;
- residual profit participation.

The same capital contribution must not be counted as both a fully repaid principal and a permanent contribution share unless the contract explicitly creates both claims. A lender, guarantor, equity investor and equipment lessor have different risk and rights profiles.

## 5. Distribution principles: a plural framework

No single ethical theory resolves every distribution question. The design must disclose which principle governs which pool.

### 5.1 Equality and equal division

Equal division is simple, legible and can express equal membership or equal standing. It may be suitable for a collective dividend from an asset whose constitution grants equal member shares.

Limitations:
- it may ignore different needs or obligations;
- it may under-reward scarce skills or exceptional risk;
- equal membership does not imply equal ownership of every project asset;
- equal shares can still be unequal in effective opportunity when participants face different barriers.

### 5.2 Need and sufficiency

Needs-based allocation prioritizes essential capabilities or a minimum threshold. It is appropriate for rights-based guarantees, emergency support and certain common services, but should not be disguised as a project contribution reward.

A sufficiency rule asks whether everyone reaches a defined adequate threshold. The threshold itself requires public justification, periodic review and a clear funding source.

### 5.3 Prioritarian and least-advantaged tests

A prioritarian rule gives greater weight to improvements for people who are worse off. A Rawlsian institutional test asks whether inequalities in the basic structure improve the position of the least advantaged while protecting equal liberties and fair opportunity. These are not equivalent to simply maximizing a weighted score or applying a universal maximin formula to every project.

SYNERGY can use least-advantaged impact as a constitutional evaluation test for institutional design without claiming that every private contract must distribute every dollar according to one formula.

### 5.4 Capability and effective opportunity

Equal resources do not always create equal real opportunities. People differ in circumstances, care responsibilities, accessibility needs, local infrastructure and ability to convert resources into meaningful choices.

UEP and UOF should therefore measure not only nominal allocations but also whether participants can actually access equipment, training, finance, markets, information and governance.

### 5.5 Entitlement and contract

A contractual or property-rights approach emphasizes legitimate acquisition, voluntary agreement, predictability and protection from arbitrary confiscation. Its validity depends on genuine consent, enforceability, lawful formation, disclosure, bargaining conditions and the broader institutional framework.

A contract does not become fair merely because it was digitally signed. Power asymmetry, information asymmetry, coercion and externalities still require scrutiny.

### 5.6 Contribution and incentive

Contribution-based rewards can support effort, innovation, reliability and accountability. They are appropriate only where:
- the outcome is defined;
- relevant contributions are observable with reasonable confidence;
- the scoring rules are disclosed before performance;
- participants can contest evidence and calculations;
- the reward pool is finite and correctly accounted for;
- basic rights and access are not conditioned on the score.

### 5.7 Bargaining and cooperative game theory

Nash bargaining, Shapley values, nucleolus and related cooperative-game solutions can help analyze surplus sharing under explicit assumptions. They do not produce a morally neutral answer.

The Shapley value allocates a transferable-utility coalition game's worth using average marginal contributions over coalition orderings. Its result depends on the coalition-value function, player definition, transferable-utility assumption and treatment of externalities. If coalition values are poorly identified, if the grand coalition is infeasible, or if externalities are omitted, the result may be misleading. Exact computation also becomes expensive as the number of players grows; approximations introduce sampling error.

Use Shapley analysis as one diagnostic or negotiated reference point, not as an automatic default distribution law. Report its assumptions, sensitivity and uncertainty.

## 6. A family of distributive mechanisms

### 6.1 Mechanism A — Contractual waterfall

Payments follow pre-agreed claims: wages, fees, supplier obligations, debt service, capital compensation, reserves and residual distribution. It is appropriate where claims are clear and legal certainty matters.

Risks: unequal bargaining power, incomplete contracts, hidden externalities and lock-in.

### 6.2 Mechanism B — Equal membership dividend

A defined share of collective surplus is distributed equally among eligible members.

\[
D_i=\frac{\alpha S_c}{N}
\]

where \(S_c\) is the verified distributable collective pool, \(N\) is the eligible member count and \(\alpha\) is the share allocated to the member dividend. Eligibility, snapshot date, membership duration, taxes and reserve policy must be explicit.

### 6.3 Mechanism C — Ownership-weighted distribution

\[
D_i=S_r\frac{o_i}{\sum_j o_j}
\]

where \(o_i\) is the valid ownership share and \(S_r\) the residual pool assigned to owners. This mechanism respects ownership claims but does not by itself answer whether the ownership structure is just or whether external costs are covered.

### 6.4 Mechanism D — Contribution-weighted project reward

\[
D_i=S_p\frac{c_i}{\sum_j c_j}
\]

This is appropriate only for a pre-defined project reward pool where contribution measures are valid and contestable. It should not be used to determine ownership title or universal income.

### 6.5 Mechanism E — Hybrid distribution

A project can constitutionally divide its residual pool into sub-pools:

\[
S_r=S_{equal}+S_{ownership}+S_{contribution}+S_{needs}+S_{collective}
\]

Each pool has its own eligible recipients and rules. The shares must sum to no more than the distributable residual. This approach makes plural principles explicit instead of hiding them in one opaque score.

### 6.6 Mechanism F — Bargaining or cooperative-game allocation

A negotiated solution may use Nash bargaining or a cooperative-game value as a reference. Participants should see:
- the coalition-value function;
- the outside options and disagreement point;
- the assumed feasible coalitions;
- externalities;
- sensitivity to player grouping;
- computation method and approximation error.

A solution is legitimate only if the process, rights and evidence are legitimate.

### 6.7 Mechanism comparison

| Mechanism | Strongest use case | Main risk | Required safeguard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contractual waterfall | Clear claims and predictable obligations | Bargaining imbalance | Disclosure, legal review, exit and dispute rights |
| Equal dividend | Shared membership asset | Free-riding or arbitrary eligibility | Transparent membership rules |
| Ownership-weighted | Residual rights of owners | Concentrated ownership | Governance and competition safeguards |
| Contribution-weighted | Measurable project reward | Metric gaming and invisible work | Audit, appeal, uncertainty bands |
| Needs-based | Basic guarantees and access | Political capture or stigma | Rights-based criteria and due process |
| Hybrid | Multi-purpose consortium | Complexity and opacity | Separate pools and public formula |
| Cooperative-game | Joint surplus analysis | Model dependence and computational cost | Sensitivity analysis and non-automatic status |

## 7. SCI — SYNERGY Contribution Index

### 7.1 Purpose and boundary

SCI is a **project-specific contribution evidence index** intended to summarize selected dimensions of contribution for a defined project and period. It is not:
- a measure of human worth;
- a universal reputation score;
- a credit score;
- a legal ownership register;
- a substitute for wages or contract;
- a measure of all social value;
- an automatic entitlement to a fixed share of profit.

### 7.2 Seven dimensions

A candidate SCI has seven dimensions, each defined with project-specific indicators:

1. **Output:** accepted deliverables or measurable production outcomes.
2. **Quality:** durability, accuracy, safety and conformity to agreed standards.
3. **Efficiency:** resource use relative to an agreed benchmark, adjusted for task complexity.
4. **Innovation:** validated improvements, learning and useful novelty.
5. **Cooperation:** contribution to shared tasks, knowledge transfer and dependency resolution.
6. **Reliability:** continuity, timely performance, maintenance and fulfilment of commitments.
7. **Risk stewardship:** prevention, disclosure and responsible management of project risk.

Risk stewardship is not identical to financial risk-bearing. A person who invests capital is not automatically scored as having performed operational risk prevention; a worker who performs dangerous work is not automatically responsible for managing the enterprise's financial risk.

### 7.3 Candidate formula

\[
SCI_i=\sum_{j=1}^{7}w_j z_{ij},\qquad
w_j\geq0,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{7}w_j=1
\]

where \(z_{ij}\) is the normalized indicator for participant \(i\) on dimension \(j\), and \(w_j\) is the project-specific weight approved before the measurement period.

This formula is a candidate aggregation method, not a validated universal index. Weights must be selected through a documented process, justified against the project's objective and tested for sensitivity. Normalization must be robust to outliers and must not silently turn a relative ranking into a claim of absolute productivity.

### 7.4 Mandatory design rules

- Publish indicators, weights, data sources and calculation code before the period begins.
- Keep raw evidence separate from normalized scores.
- Report confidence intervals or uncertainty bands where feasible.
- Show missingness and data-quality flags; never silently impute high scores.
- Permit corrections, explanations, independent review and appeal.
- Prevent one dimension from being counted twice under different labels.
- Use role-adjusted and task-complexity-adjusted benchmarks where defensible.
- Do not reward unsafe speed or output achieved by shifting costs to others.
- Do not punish lawful absence, disability, caregiving or protected activity.
- Do not sell or expose personal data beyond the declared lawful purpose.
- Revalidate weights when the project, technology or task mix changes.
- Retain an auditable history of rule changes and retroactive corrections.

### 7.5 Better than a single score: report a profile

Whenever possible, display a seven-dimensional profile and evidence quality, not only a scalar. A scalar may be useful for a narrow, pre-agreed calculation but can conceal trade-offs: a high output score must not erase serious safety failures or a low output score caused by a participant's assigned maintenance role.

A score should be considered **non-comparable** across projects unless definitions, scales, time windows, role composition and measurement quality are demonstrably aligned.

### 7.6 Anti-gaming and Goodhart controls

Potential attacks include:
- maximizing easily counted tasks while neglecting hard tasks;
- splitting one task into many counted events;
- colluding to inflate peer ratings;
- hiding failures until after the measurement window;
- excluding difficult participants or clients;
- manipulating baselines;
- generating artificial activity through Sybil identities;
- suppressing dissent or safety reports;
- transferring costs to another cell or period.

Controls include random independent audits, anomaly detection, identity and eligibility checks proportionate to risk, outcome sampling, rotating reviewers, protected whistleblowing, adversarial testing and a human appeal path. Automated fraud flags must trigger review, not automatic guilt.

## 8. UEP — Universal Economic Participation

### 8.1 Definition

UEP is a proposed institutional architecture for broadening effective access to economic participation. It is not a token distribution event and not a guarantee that every participant will receive equal investment returns.

It has four distinct pillars:

1. **Basic guarantees:** rights-based access to essential protections and services, defined by applicable law and public or collective institutions.
2. **Productive assets:** access to tools, equipment, infrastructure, software, workspace, data resources and shared capital.
3. **Verified-contribution rewards:** transparent project-specific compensation under disclosed rules.
4. **Collective results:** participation in returns generated by genuinely shared assets, networks, knowledge pools or common infrastructure.

These pillars have different funding sources, legal bases, risk profiles and eligibility rules. They must be accounted for separately.

### 8.2 UEP access pathway

A participant should be able to:
- discover opportunities and requirements;
- verify eligibility without excessive data collection;
- access training and capability support;
- apply for equipment or shared infrastructure;
- join projects voluntarily;
- understand compensation and ownership terms before joining;
- receive a clear record of work and claims;
- challenge attribution and payment decisions;
- exit without forfeiting unrelated basic rights.

### 8.3 Universal does not mean identical

Universal participation means that the system is designed for open, fair and non-arbitrary access under transparent criteria. It does not mean identical allocations regardless of need, capacity, project risk, legal constraints or resource scarcity.

The system must measure effective access: who can actually use the offered resources, who is excluded by geography, disability, care duties, connectivity, language, collateral requirements or hidden fees.

### 8.4 UEP outcome measures

Candidate measures:
- share of eligible participants who can access productive assets;
- time from application to decision;
- participation and completion rates by relevant access barriers;
- productive utilization and idle time of shared assets;
- project survival and additional output;
- participant income and wealth changes, separately measured;
- ownership participation and concentration;
- grievance and appeal resolution time;
- default, loss and exclusion rates;
- net outcomes after displacement and subsidy costs.

Disaggregation must respect privacy and applicable anti-discrimination law.

## 9. UOF — Universal Opportunity Fund

### 9.1 Mission

UOF is a proposed financing and capability institution that lowers barriers to productive participation. Its mandate is to expand opportunity and productive capacity, not to maximize token circulation or make every applicant a borrower.

Possible uses:
- access to tools and equipment;
- training and certification;
- shared infrastructure and workspaces;
- collective procurement;
- initial project capital;
- market access and partner discovery;
- research, prototyping and validation;
- accessibility adaptations;
- emergency continuity support where it protects productive capability.

### 9.2 Allocation model

A candidate needs-and-opportunity function is:

\[
UOF_i=f(N_i,I_i,A_i,R_i,E_i)
\]

where:
- \(N_i\): demonstrated need or access barrier;
- \(I_i\): expected additionality of the intervention;
- \(A_i\): feasibility and readiness;
- \(R_i\): risk, including downside and concentration risk;
- \(E_i\): expected economic, capability and social/ecological outcomes.

These variables must not be collapsed into a single opaque AI score. A practical decision model may use eligibility gates, a transparent rubric, a portfolio constraint and human review. Need and basic rights must not be treated as equivalent to expected financial return.

### 9.3 Instrument choice

UOF can use different instruments depending on the project:
- grants for public-good, access or early capability investments;
- loans where repayment capacity and fair terms exist;
- revenue-based finance where cash flows are measurable and risk is shared;
- equipment leasing or shared-use pools;
- guarantees with capped exposure;
- equity or cooperative ownership where appropriate and lawful;
- procurement commitments or advance purchase agreements;
- training vouchers and direct service provision.

The fund should not force debt onto participants who cannot reasonably repay. Nor should it disguise equity risk as a guaranteed savings product.

### 9.4 Fund compartments

A possible compartment structure:
1. **Access and capability window:** training, accessibility, basic equipment and onboarding.
2. **Productive asset window:** shared tools, machinery, software and infrastructure.
3. **Seed and experimentation window:** prototypes and early-stage projects.
4. **Collective procurement window:** purchasing power and supply-chain resilience.
5. **Market access window:** certification, logistics, distribution and partner integration.
6. **Resilience window:** maintenance, continuity, emergency repair and shock response.

Each compartment needs a separate budget, risk appetite, eligibility rule, performance measure and loss policy.

### 9.5 Governance and safeguards

- Independent investment and impact review.
- Conflict-of-interest declarations and recusals.
- Public reporting of aggregate allocation and outcomes.
- Clear separation of grants, loans, guarantees and equity.
- Exposure limits by participant, sector, geography and correlated risk.
- Transparent pricing, repayment and default policies.
- No hidden recourse to basic guarantees or unrelated personal assets.
- Appeals for procedural error and discrimination.
- External audit and periodic mandate review.
- No AI authority to change constitutional limits or create unapproved liabilities.

### 9.6 Additionality

A fund should estimate whether its intervention changed the outcome relative to a credible alternative. If the same project would have obtained the same financing on similar terms, the fund's additionality may be low. If support displaces private finance or subsidizes insiders, reported gross outputs can exaggerate impact.

## 10. Collective dividends and shared assets

### 10.1 Four collective dividend classes

A consortium may create distinct collective dividend pools:

1. **Collective Asset Dividend:** returns from jointly owned productive assets.
2. **Network Dividend:** returns from shared infrastructure, interoperability or network services.
3. **Knowledge Dividend:** income from licensed collective knowledge, software, data products or intellectual property, subject to consent and rights.
4. **Social Impact Dividend:** a budgeted allocation from verified surplus to agreed community or ecological outcomes.

These pools must not be confused. A social impact budget is not necessarily a dividend claim; a network fee is not automatically profit; a knowledge dividend must respect ownership, licensing, privacy and consent.

### 10.2 Eligible base

Before a dividend is declared, calculate:
- realized income attributable to the asset or pool;
- operating and maintenance expenses;
- taxes and legal obligations;
- debt and senior claims;
- replacement and resilience reserves;
- retained earnings required by the asset constitution;
- externality remediation and restoration obligations where applicable.

Only the residual amount may be declared distributable.

### 10.3 Distribution options

A collective constitution may select:
- equal member distribution;
- ownership-share distribution;
- usage-based rebate;
- contribution-based reward;
- needs-based access allocation;
- reinvestment into the shared asset;
- a hybrid of the above.

Usage-based rebates should not be confused with ownership dividends. A participant who uses an asset more may receive a usage rebate without acquiring more ownership.

### 10.4 Anti-capture

Shared assets can be captured by early members, large capital providers, administrators or the platform operator. Safeguards include:
- caps or graduated voting rules where legally appropriate;
- member representation and independent oversight;
- transparent related-party transactions;
- open access criteria;
- exit and portability;
- periodic review of fee and dividend policy;
- protection of minority members;
- public-purpose covenants for assets financed by public or community funds.

## 11. A formal hybrid allocation model

### 11.1 Separate the pools first

Let \(S\) be verified distributable residual surplus after mandatory claims, operating costs, taxes, due liabilities and approved reserves. A constitution partitions it:

\[
S=S_E+S_O+S_C+S_N+S_K
\]

where:
- \(S_E\): equal membership pool;
- \(S_O\): ownership-rights pool;
- \(S_C\): project contribution pool;
- \(S_N\): needs/access pool;
- \(S_K\): collective reinvestment or common-good pool.

All pool shares are non-negative and:

\[
\alpha_E+\alpha_O+\alpha_C+\alpha_N+\alpha_K\leq1
\]

with \(S_q=\alpha_qS\), and any undistributed remainder retained under the reserve policy.

### 11.2 Participant allocation

For participant \(i\), a candidate allocation is:

\[
D_i=
\frac{S_E}{N_E}
+S_O\frac{o_i}{\sum_j o_j}
+S_C\frac{c_i}{\sum_j c_j}
+S_N\frac{n_i}{\sum_j n_j}
\]

where each term applies only to the eligible population for that pool. The collective reinvestment pool \(S_K\) remains with the collective asset or is spent under its mandate; it is not automatically distributed to individuals.

This equation is a bookkeeping template, not a moral theorem. Eligibility sets, weights, caps, minimums and pool proportions must be approved and disclosed before the period. If a denominator is zero, the corresponding pool is not silently redistributed; the constitution specifies whether it is retained, reallocated or returned.

### 11.3 Constraints

A valid allocation must satisfy:
- budget balance;
- no payment from unrecognized or unavailable funds;
- compliance with law and enforceable claims;
- non-discrimination and due process;
- solvency and reserve requirements;
- disclosed eligibility and calculation;
- bounded exposure to measurement error;
- no double-counting across pools;
- protection of basic rights;
- explicit treatment of externalities and intergenerational costs.

### 11.4 Uncertainty-aware contribution weights

Instead of treating \(c_i\) as a precise point estimate, represent it as an interval or distribution:

\[
c_i\in[\underline c_i,\overline c_i]
\]

or \(c_i\sim P_i\) where evidence supports a probabilistic model. Report allocation sensitivity under plausible values. If small changes in assumptions cause large payment changes, the system should use a negotiated or capped allocation rather than false precision.

### 11.5 Counterfactual and joint surplus

For a project with participants \(N\), define a coalition value function \(v(A)\) only where the coalition and outcome are meaningfully specified. The total cooperative surplus relative to a baseline may be:

\[
S_{joint}=v(N)-v(\varnothing)-C_{coord}-C_{integration}-C_{external}-C_{risk}
\]

This is not automatically equal to accounting profit. It is an analytical counterfactual estimate that requires a defensible model, and it must not be added to the financial surplus ledger as if it were cash.

## 12. Fairness, legitimacy and governance

### 12.1 Procedural legitimacy

A distribution rule is not legitimate merely because it is mathematically elegant. It needs:
- advance disclosure;
- meaningful participation in rule formation;
- consent where required;
- clear jurisdiction and applicable law;
- independent audit;
- access to underlying evidence, subject to privacy;
- reasoned decisions;
- appeal and correction;
- protection from retaliation;
- periodic review.

### 12.2 Voice, exit and contestability

A participant should know:
- which body made the decision;
- which rule and data were applied;
- how to challenge errors;
- whether an independent reviewer is available;
- what exit does to accrued claims;
- whether personal data can be exported, corrected or deleted under applicable law.

Exit must not erase earned wages or valid accrued claims. Nor should it automatically preserve a right to future distributions where the participant has lawfully transferred or surrendered the relevant ownership interest.

### 12.3 Distributional incidence

Evaluate not only who receives the nominal payment, but who ultimately bears costs and captures benefits. For every mechanism, test:
- effects by income and wealth position;
- access barriers and disability;
- gendered and unpaid care impacts where lawful and ethically appropriate to study;
- geography and infrastructure access;
- concentration of voting and ownership;
- intergenerational effects;
- ecological burden;
- transfers between cells and external parties.

### 12.4 Privacy-preserving accountability

Use data minimization, purpose limitation, role-based access, retention limits and privacy-preserving aggregation. Public transparency should normally concern rules, aggregate flows, audits and institutional decisions—not unrestricted publication of personal work histories or sensitive data.

## 13. Failure modes and adversarial scenarios

| Failure mode | Mechanism | Observable warning | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goodhart effect | Participants optimize the measured indicator | Metric rises while outcome quality falls | Multi-metric review, random audits, periodic redesign |
| Contribution double-counting | Same joint effect credited to several actors | Total attributed contribution exceeds defensible value | Separate direct and interaction effects |
| Ownership capture | Voting or assets concentrate | Related-party control and persistent minority exclusion | Caps, disclosure, independent governance |
| Metric discrimination | Proxy variables encode unequal access | Systematic unexplained error across groups | Impact assessment, lawful review, appeal |
| Sybil manipulation | Fake identities inflate membership or rewards | Abnormal identity and activity patterns | Proportionate verification and audit |
| Collusive scoring | Participants exchange inflated ratings | Reciprocal rating clusters | Independent evidence and rotation |
| Care invisibility | Only market transactions are counted | Essential unpaid work omitted | Explicit care and capability indicators |
| Risk dumping | Losses shifted to workers, public or future periods | Defaults, unpaid claims, ecological damage | Full liability and externality accounting |
| Token illusion | Token balance mistaken for real claim or wealth | Redemption and legal backing unclear | Rights registry and independent legal review |
| AI capture | Model operator changes weights or eligibility | Unexplained rule drift | Versioning, approval gates and audit |
| Short-termism | Reward horizon too short | Maintenance and resilience decline | Deferred outcomes and lifecycle measures |
| Fund adverse selection | Applicants conceal risk or strongest applicants dominate | Defaults or exclusion concentration | Portfolio design and fair access review |

A red-team test must attempt to exploit each mechanism before deployment. The test should include insiders, administrators, large capital holders, colluding participants, malicious identities, data providers and model operators.

## 14. Empirical and computational research programme

### 14.1 Comparative simulation

Build an agent-based and accounting-consistent simulator with heterogeneous agents:
- workers and specialists;
- entrepreneurs and managers;
- capital providers;
- asset owners;
- consumers and community members;
- public institutions;
- common-resource stewards.

Each agent has resources, skills, needs, outside options, risk tolerance, access barriers and a bounded strategy. Firms and cells produce using complementary inputs, incur costs, create externalities and may fail.

### 14.2 Mechanisms to compare

At minimum:
1. equal split;
2. ownership-weighted split;
3. contribution-weighted split;
4. needs-based allocation;
5. contract-first waterfall;
6. hybrid multi-pool allocation;
7. Nash bargaining benchmark;
8. Shapley benchmark under explicit coalition values;
9. equal-division/Shapley hybrid;
10. UOF grants, loans and shared-asset access under different eligibility rules.

### 14.3 Experimental factors

Vary:
- complementarity strength;
- measurement noise and missing data;
- bargaining-power asymmetry;
- concentration of initial ownership;
- access to capital and productive assets;
- time horizon;
- externality magnitude;
- volatility and shocks;
- coalition size;
- network effects;
- ability to exit;
- fraud and collusion;
- governance participation;
- AI recommendation quality and model drift.

### 14.4 Outcome metrics

Report separately:
- total output and value added;
- net distributable cash and solvency;
- productivity and asset utilization;
- median and lower-quantile income;
- wealth and ownership concentration;
- effective access to productive assets;
- participation and retention;
- innovation and survival;
- unpaid care recognition;
- grievance incidence and resolution;
- ecological pressure and restoration;
- volatility, resilience and recovery time;
- administrative and verification costs.

Do not combine these into one “success score” without a publicly defended normative weighting. Present Pareto frontiers and trade-offs.

### 14.5 Identification strategy

Where possible, use randomized pilots, phased rollouts, matched comparison groups, difference-in-differences with defensible assumptions, regression discontinuity at genuine eligibility thresholds, and qualitative process tracing. Pre-register hypotheses and primary outcomes. Publish negative results and deviations from the original protocol.

## 15. Falsifiable hypotheses

- **H3-1 — Hybrid allocation:** In projects with heterogeneous contributions and shared assets, a transparent multi-pool mechanism may improve perceived procedural fairness without materially reducing productive incentives compared with a single contribution-weighted rule. This must be tested; it is not assumed.
- **H3-2 — Metric transparency:** Advance disclosure of indicators, weights and appeal rights will reduce disputes about attribution relative to opaque scoring, controlling for project type and outcome.
- **H3-3 — Uncertainty display:** Showing confidence intervals and evidence quality will reduce false precision and improve correction rates, though it may increase administrative cost.
- **H3-4 — Complementarity:** The gap between additive attribution and interaction-aware attribution will increase with input complementarity.
- **H3-5 — Care visibility:** Explicitly recording maintenance, training and care-related inputs will change measured contribution distributions relative to transaction-only accounting.
- **H3-6 — UEP access:** Access to shared productive assets will increase participation among previously constrained groups only where complementary capabilities, markets and support are also available.
- **H3-7 — UOF additionality:** Opportunity-fund support will produce higher net additional output when allocation accounts for access barriers and counterfactual financing availability than when it selects applicants solely by projected financial return.
- **H3-8 — Ownership concentration:** Collective dividends without governance and anti-capture safeguards will not reliably prevent concentration of control.
- **H3-9 — Shapley sensitivity:** Shapley allocations will vary materially when coalition-value functions, player grouping or externalities are changed in settings with strong complementarity.
- **H3-10 — Appeal systems:** Independent appeal mechanisms will identify a measurable share of attribution or eligibility errors and improve trust, at a quantifiable cost.
- **H3-11 — Long horizons:** Deferred and lifecycle-sensitive measures will increase recognition of maintenance and resilience contributions relative to short-period output metrics.
- **H3-12 — Hybrid resilience:** Under shocks and heterogeneous outside options, hybrid distribution may improve retention or recovery in some settings, but can underperform simpler mechanisms where administrative costs exceed its benefits.

Every hypothesis must define population, treatment, comparison, outcome, measurement window and failure criterion before data collection.

## 16. Implementation sequence

### Phase 1 — Accounting and rights map
- identify legal entities and system boundaries;
- map assets, liabilities, claims and data controllers;
- separate cash, accounting profit, token balances and estimated social value;
- define dispute jurisdiction and participant rights.

### Phase 2 — Pilot contribution ledger
- select one bounded project;
- define seven SCI dimensions only where relevant;
- publish indicators and weights;
- preserve raw evidence and uncertainty;
- run a shadow calculation without financial consequences.

### Phase 3 — Shadow distribution comparison
- calculate multiple candidate allocations;
- compare outcomes and participant perceptions;
- perform sensitivity, bias and gaming tests;
- publish distributional incidence.

### Phase 4 — Limited financial pilot
- use capped pools and explicit contracts;
- independent audit and appeal;
- no automatic AI execution of disputed payments;
- monitor solvency, claims and externalities.

### Phase 5 — UEP/UOF institutional pilot
- test access and productive-asset utilization;
- separate grant, debt, lease and equity instruments;
- evaluate additionality and default;
- protect basic rights from financial recourse.

### Phase 6 — Replication and constitutional review
- replicate across different sectors and cell sizes;
- publish code, assumptions and negative findings;
- revise the constitution only through authorized processes;
- prohibit silent changes to historical claims.

## 20. Research status and limitations

This volume is a structured research proposal, not an empirically validated distributive constitution. The SCI dimensions and UEP/UOF architecture are candidate designs. The hybrid equations are accounting and simulation templates, not universal justice formulas. No empirical claim is made that one allocation mechanism will outperform all alternatives.

Before real deployment, each mechanism requires:
- jurisdiction-specific legal review;
- accounting and tax review;
- security, privacy and data-protection assessment;
- adversarial testing;
- participant consultation;
- pilot evidence;
- independent audit;
- explicit procedures for correction, appeal and exit.

**The governing principle is separation with accountability:** measure contribution without ranking human worth; recognize ownership without assuming it proves contribution; distribute surplus without confusing it with gross activity; expand opportunity without turning support into coercive debt; and use AI to assist decisions without giving it unbounded authority.
