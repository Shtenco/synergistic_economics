## Abstract

Volume 0 and Volume I were developed for different but related purposes. Volume I states twelve broad constraints and premises about economic reality. Volume 0 then proposed twelve additional statements under the label “axioms”. A direct comparison shows that these lists are not two independent sets of foundational axioms. They overlap, operate at different logical levels and include different kinds of statements.

This addendum establishes a hierarchy:

1. **Ontological and behavioural premises** describe recurring features of the economic world (scarcity, dispersed knowledge, heterogeneity, interdependence, institutions, incentives, uncertainty, power, external effects, learning and intergenerational continuity).
2. **Normative commitments** specify what SYNERGY must protect (human dignity, rights, due process and legitimate agency).
3. **Derived design principles** specify how institutions should be designed under those premises and commitments.
4. **Accounting and operational invariants** specify what a valid implementation must not confuse or violate.
5. **Empirical hypotheses** make contingent claims that can be tested and rejected.

The twelve A-axioms in Volume I remain the canonical foundational set for the current research programme. The twelve S-statements in Volume 0 are retained but reclassified as a proposed set of constitutional/design commitments and research rules, mapped to the A-axioms. They must not be counted as a second independent axiom system.

This is a working reconciliation, not a final philosophical proof. It makes the project more internally consistent and clarifies which statements are normative, descriptive, operational or empirical.

# Part I — The logical architecture

## 1. Five levels that must not be conflated

| Level | What it states | Example | How it is evaluated |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1. Ontological / behavioural premise | A feature of economic reality or human interaction | Knowledge is dispersed; resources are finite | Conceptual adequacy, observation and counterexamples |
| L2. Normative commitment | A value or right that the system chooses to protect | Basic rights are not conditional on productivity | Ethical justification, legitimacy and constitutional review |
| L3. Design principle | A rule for selecting or structuring institutions | Use polycentric governance where spillovers and local knowledge justify it | Comparative institutional performance and rights compliance |
| L4. Operational invariant | A condition that implementation and accounting must preserve | Credit principal is not consolidated income | Accounting consistency, audit and system tests |
| L5. Empirical hypothesis | A contingent causal or predictive claim | Shared reserves reduce losses under specified conditions | Falsifiable comparative evidence |

A statement may connect more than one level, but its primary logical role must be declared. For example, “human dignity is non-negotiable” is a normative commitment; it is not an empirical regularity. “People respond to incentives” is a behavioural premise; it does not itself dictate one specific reward system. “A particular contribution score improves productivity” is an empirical hypothesis and must be tested.

## 2. Canonical status of the two existing lists

**Volume I A1–A12:** canonical foundational premises and constraints for the current version of SYNERGY. They describe the conditions the design must take seriously. A11 is explicitly normative (rights and dignity) and should be understood as a constitutional commitment within the foundational set.

**Volume 0 S1–S12:** reclassified as *constitutional/design commitments and research rules*. They summarize how SYNERGY intends to respond to the foundational premises. They are not twelve additional ontological axioms.

**Volume I D1–D10:** derived design principles. They translate the foundational premises into institutional requirements.

The correct dependency is:

\[
\text{A-premises and rights commitments}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{S-design commitments}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{D-design principles}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{mechanisms and implementation}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{tests and revision}.
\]

This is a conceptual dependency graph, not a claim that every principle follows deductively from one axiom without additional normative assumptions.

## 4. What does not follow automatically

Several mappings require an explicit bridge premise:

- **Institutional plurality does not imply institutional equivalence.** The fact that multiple forms exist does not establish that they perform equally well. Comparative evidence is required.
- **Interdependence does not imply positive synergy.** Complementarity can be outweighed by coordination costs, congestion, rent extraction or contagion.
- **Dispersed knowledge does not imply that all decisions should be decentralized.** Shared infrastructure, systemic risks and cross-boundary externalities can justify federation or public coordination.
- **Human heterogeneity does not by itself determine a distributive formula.** Rights and distributive principles require ethical and constitutional justification.
- **Financial integrity does not establish a preferred currency or monetary architecture.** It establishes reconciliation requirements and distinctions between claims, settlement assets and real resources.
- **AI capability does not establish AI legitimacy.** Authority must be delegated, bounded, auditable and contestable.
- **A positive simulation does not establish real-world superiority.** Identification, calibration, external validity and replication remain necessary.

# Part III — Corrected terminology and key distinctions

## 5. Value, price, utility, surplus, profit, income and wealth

These terms must not be used interchangeably.

### 5.1 Price

A price is a monetary or otherwise specified exchange ratio observed or agreed under a particular market, contract, institution and time. A price is evidence about an exchange, not a complete measure of social value.

### 5.2 Utility and preference

Utility is a representation of preference or well-being within a specified theory. In many economic models it is ordinal: the ranking of alternatives matters, not an independently measurable quantity of happiness. Interpersonal comparison requires additional assumptions.

### 5.3 Labour time and Marxian value categories

Socially necessary labour time is a theoretical category within Marxian political economy. It is not identical to market price, accounting cost, hours personally spent or an observed universal unit of social contribution. The relation between value categories and prices is a substantive theoretical debate.

### 5.4 Production and output

Production is an organized transformation of inputs into outputs. Gross output may count intermediate transactions multiple times; value added subtracts intermediate consumption under national-accounting conventions. Neither gross output nor value added alone captures all external effects or capabilities.

### 5.5 Surplus

“Surplus” has multiple meanings. It may refer to producer/consumer surplus in welfare economics, operating surplus in national accounts, accounting retained earnings, residual income after specified costs, or surplus value in Marxian theory. Every use must name its definition.

### 5.6 Profit

Accounting profit is revenue minus recognized expenses under a stated accounting standard and reporting boundary. Economic profit may additionally account for opportunity costs. Neither is identical to social surplus or net social value.

### 5.7 Income and wealth

Income is a flow over a period. Wealth is a stock measured at a point in time, typically assets less liabilities under a specified valuation and consolidation boundary. A rise in asset prices can increase measured wealth without a corresponding increase in productive capacity.

### 5.8 Capital

Capital may mean produced productive assets, financial claims, equity, social relationships or a theoretical value category depending on the discipline. Every model must specify which meaning it uses. Financial capital is not automatically a physical productive resource.

### 5.9 Prosperity

Prosperity is a normative and multidimensional concept. It may include income, security, capabilities, health, agency, ownership, resilience and ecological conditions. The choice and weighting of dimensions must be disclosed.

## 6. Accounting and financial invariants

The following distinctions are mandatory across all SYNERGY research and software:

1. Issuance of a token or monetary liability is not, by itself, production of real wealth.
2. Credit principal is a financial claim and liability pair at origination; it is not consolidated income.
3. An internal transfer between entities inside a consolidated boundary is not consolidated revenue.
4. Gross transaction volume is not value added.
5. Total value locked is not unencumbered net asset value.
6. Collateral value is not the same as liquidity or solvency.
7. A payment instruction is not necessarily settlement finality.
8. A ledger record is not, by itself, proof of legal title, physical delivery or valid consent.
9. A forecast is not an observed fact; an estimate is not a verified event.
10. A social or ecological benefit must not be counted as cash unless an enforceable cash flow exists.

These are not claims that money and finance are unimportant. They are requirements to prevent category errors and double counting.

# Part IV — Revisions to the axiom architecture

## 7. Canonical foundational set

For the current research version, retain Volume I's twelve A-axioms as the single canonical list:

A1 finite resources; A2 dispersed knowledge; A3 human heterogeneity; A4 interdependence; A5 institutional mediation; A6 incentive responsiveness; A7 uncertainty; A8 power asymmetry; A9 external effects; A10 dynamic learning; A11 human dignity and rights; A12 intergenerational continuity.

Do not add S1–S12 to this list as if they were twelve more axioms. Keep them as the design and constitutional response layer.

## 8. Add two missing derived design principles to Volume I

The current Volume I design principles D1–D10 already cover polycentric coordination, mechanism pluralism, rights-constrained optimization, productive opportunity, accountable ownership, stewardship, adaptive institutions, contestable power, multidimensional performance and evidence before scale.

Two important operational requirements deserve explicit status:

### D11 — Financial and accounting integrity

**Derived from:** A1, A5, A6, A7, A9 and A12.

Every financial mechanism must distinguish issuance, deposits, credit, payment instructions, settlement assets, liquidity, solvency, income, wealth and real productive capacity. Consolidation boundaries, collateral, maturity, loss allocation and recognition rules must be explicit. No internal transfer, new liability or token issuance may be presented as net real wealth creation without an independently evidenced increase in the underlying asset base or productive capacity.

**Test:** Reconcile entity-level and consolidated balance sheets, cash flows, claims and obligations. Verify that intercompany transfers, credit principal and token issuance are not double-counted as output or net wealth.

### D12 — Bounded and contestable computational authority

**Derived from:** A2, A7, A8, A10 and A11.

AI and automated systems may operate only within a documented mandate, defined action space, risk limits, data permissions and reversible execution boundaries. Recommendation, authorization, execution, audit and appeal must be separable for consequential actions. Affected participants must have a route to explanation and review.

**Test:** Red-team model failure, data manipulation, objective gaming, unauthorized action and concentrated control. Verify logs, permission boundaries, override and recovery procedures.

These principles should be added to Volume I's existing D1–D10 list, not promoted to independent axioms.

# Part V — Methodological upgrades to the research programme

## 9. Avoiding false synthesis

A successful synthesis must not:
- declare all schools “partly right” without specifying conditions;
- combine incompatible definitions in one equation;
- treat a historical author's metaphor as a formal theorem;
- treat an accounting identity as a causal theory;
- treat equilibrium existence as proof of stability or fairness;
- infer individual outcomes from aggregate gains;
- infer social welfare from market capitalization or token price;
- assume that decentralization eliminates power;
- assume that public ownership eliminates capture;
- assume that cooperation is always positive-sum;
- treat AI prediction as causal knowledge;
- infer real-world effectiveness from an uncalibrated simulation.

## 10. Minimum specification for every future model

Every formal model in SYNERGY should include:

1. the question and decision domain;
2. unit of analysis and system boundary;
3. state variables and units;
4. actors, heterogeneity and behavioural rules;
5. information sets and observability;
6. institutional and legal assumptions;
7. production and resource constraints;
8. financial and accounting treatment;
9. time horizon and transition dynamics;
10. objective function and hard constraints;
11. uncertainty and sensitivity analysis;
12. baseline alternatives;
13. empirical calibration and data provenance;
14. falsification and failure criteria;
15. privacy, rights and distributional impact.

A model lacking several of these may still be a useful toy model, but its claims must be limited accordingly.

## 11. Research methodology: history as a living laboratory

The comparison of schools should be iterative. Historical theories are not merely milestones to be ranked by how closely they resemble current orthodoxy. They are analytical resources whose concepts can be reconstructed, compared and tested against contemporary problems.

A recent methodological article explicitly proposes treating history of economic thought as a “living laboratory” for comparing, criticizing and developing theories. Contemporary work on ontological theorising also emphasizes that economic models carry assumptions about individuals, relations, systems, causality, money, capital and technology that should be made explicit.

For SYNERGY, this means:
- reconstruct original arguments in historical context;
- distinguish primary texts from later textbook summaries;
- compare rival theories on shared questions without erasing their different purposes;
- identify the ontology and causal mechanism behind each model;
- test modern reconstructions rather than attributing modern equations to historical authors;
- record unresolved disputes instead of forcing premature consensus.

## 12. Research workflow and change control

Every new volume must state:
- which A-axioms it uses;
- which design principles it derives;
- which concepts it defines or revises;
- which prior claims it challenges;
- what empirical evidence would change its conclusion.

If a later volume proposes a new foundational axiom, it must explain why the existing twelve are insufficient, identify whether the new statement is ontological, normative or methodological, and assess its compatibility with all existing axioms.

## Conclusion

The canonical foundation is now clearer:

- Volume I describes the foundational conditions and constraints of economic reality.
- Volume 0 compares the historical and modern theories that explain those conditions and proposes a synthesis.
- This addendum reconciles their logical levels and defines the rules for future theory-building.
- Subsequent volumes must derive models and institutional mechanisms from this foundation, then test them against credible alternatives.

The aim is not to make the theory immune to criticism. The aim is to make its claims precise enough that criticism can improve it—and evidence can prove it wrong where necessary.
